## What are goshuin?

Goshuin are the brush-written calligraphy and red seals that you receive at a shrine or temple as **proof of your visit**. They are made up of a large red seal (the seal of the shrine or temple) and brush-written calligraphy such as the name of the shrine or temple and the date of your visit.

### Goshuin are not "stamps"

The Miyagi Prefecture Shrine Association explains that goshuin are proof of a visit and only have meaning when you have actually worshipped. It also cautions against becoming too eager about collecting them, since this is not a stamp rally, and says that goshuin, like other items received from a shrine, are sacred things based on faith.

In other words, they are different in meaning from the stamps found at tourist sites. **The basic rule is to pray first, and receive the goshuin afterward.**

### The origin of goshuin

The Tokyo Shrine Association explains that the origin of goshuin may be the "nokyo uketori no kakitsuke" (a written receipt for a sutra offering) that people received in the Nara and Heian periods when they dedicated hand-copied sutras to a shrine or temple. Over time, it is thought, people came to have a certificate written simply for having visited, without making a sutra offering.

According to the Miyagi Prefecture Shrine Association, in the Edo period the practice became one in which a Shinto priest at the shrine you visited wrote the calligraphy and applied the seal. The form in which the shrine's red seal is stamped in the center, as it is today, spread in the Meiji period, and when railways developed in the Taisho and Showa periods, the enjoyment of collecting them as "travel memories" grew. The name "goshuin" came into use in the Showa period; before that they were reportedly called "shain," "insho," "gohan" and "goin."

## Shrine goshuin and temple goshuin

Goshuin exist at both shrines and temples. According to the Japan Tourism Agency's multilingual commentary database (Chichibu), until the separation of Shinto and Buddhism in 1868, the kami and the Buddha were worshipped in a blended form.

**Some people** keep separate goshuincho for shrines and for temples, but whether to separate them is a matter of personal preference. However, the same database cautions that some shrines and temples decline a goshuincho that carries seals of another religion. If you are concerned, ask a quick question when you make your request.

## How to receive a goshuin (5 steps)

1. **Pray first.** Purify yourself at the temizu basin and pray at the main hall. The Japan National Tourism Organization (JNTO) also guides visitors to pray first, before lining up.
2. **Go to the reception counter (shamusho).** Location and reception hours differ by shrine. The hours of the reception counter are often shorter than the hours you can enter the grounds, so take particular care if you arrive in the evening.
3. **Hand over your goshuincho, opened to the page where you want it written.** The Miyagi Prefecture Shrine Association recommends checking which way is up, and telling the staff in advance of any special request (such as leaving one page blank).
4. **Wait quietly.** The writer is concentrating on the work. Do not speak to them while they write, and wait quietly until you are called. When it is crowded, you may leave your book and collect it later (JNTO). It is safer to write your name on it.
5. **Pay the hatsuho-ryo (offering fee) and receive it.** Prepare small change. JNTO gives a guideline of 300 to 500 yen.

## About the goshuincho and "kakioki"

### Where can you get a goshuincho?

Many shrines offer their own original goshuincho. Examples confirmed on official websites are as follows.

- Meiji Jingu: 1,500 yen (official English page)
- Kitano Tenmangu: 2,000 yen (4 colors; offerings page)
- Kifune Jinja: 2,000 yen (offerings page)

Goshuincho are also sold at bookstores, general-goods shops and elsewhere, but this article introduces only what could be confirmed in the official guidance of shrines.

### What is kakioki (a pre-written paper goshuin)?

Kakioki is a goshuin that has been written on paper in advance. If you do not have a goshuincho, you may be able to receive one on paper. The Miyagi Prefecture Shrine Association also explains that some shrines will give you one on paper if you have forgotten your book. You can also paste it into your book later with glue, but some shrines are not prepared for this, so do not press the request.

During crowded periods, some shrines give **kakioki only**. For example, Fushimi Inari Taisha announced that during its Reiwa 8 hatsumode period, only kakioki would be given. Meiji Jingu's "Autumn Grand Festival limited goshuin" is also given as kakioki.

## A guide to fees, hours and payment

- **Fees:** JNTO gives a guideline of about 300 to 500 yen. At Meiji Jingu, according to its official English page, a goshuin costs 500 yen. Special goshuin can cost different amounts (the special New Year offering of Sumiyoshi Taisha's Hattatsu goshuin was 1,000 yen, in Reiwa 8).
- **Hours:** These differ by shrine. Meiji Jingu announces 9:00 AM to closing time, Kitano Tenmangu 9:00 AM to 4:30 PM, Ise Jingu 6:00 AM to 6:00 PM (until 5:00 PM from October to December), and Kifune Jinja 9:00 AM to 5:00 PM. On festival days and similar occasions, reception may not be available.
- **Payment:** Prepare cash. Meiji Jingu's official English Q&A says that credit cards cannot be used at the goshuin counter. Hokkaido Jingu also says that the hatsuho-ryo for goshuin and similar items is cash only.

## What not to do (etiquette)

These are points that are raised in common in the official guidance of the Miyagi Prefecture Shrine Association, Kifune Jinja, Asakusa Jinja, Sakuragi Jinja and others.

- **Receiving only a goshuin without praying.** Kifune Jinja says that goshuin are given only to people who have visited in person, and cannot be bought at the shop for offerings.
- **Rushing the person who is writing, peering at them while they write, or photographing them without permission.**
- **Going outside the reception hours.**
- **Complaining about how it turned out.** Because it is written by a person, no two are exactly alike (Miyagi Prefecture Shrine Association).
- **Collecting them in order to resell them.** Asakusa Jinja writes that resale on internet auctions has become a problem.
- **Putting other stamps in your goshuincho.** JNTO guides visitors to avoid this, because goshuin are sacred.

## For visitors from overseas

- **Prepare cash (small change in Japanese yen).** At some shrines, cards cannot be used at the goshuin counter (official guidance of Meiji Jingu and Hokkaido Jingu).
- **Check the reception hours in advance.** The reception counter may close earlier than the visiting hours of the grounds.
- **Multilingual commentary is available.** The Japan Tourism Agency's multilingual commentary database carries explanations of goshuin in Traditional Chinese, Simplified Chinese, English and Korean (the Chichibu, Shikoku and Shoshazan commentaries).
- **Pray first, then receive the goshuin.** JNTO also guides visitors to hand over the book opened to the page they want written.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I receive a goshuin without a goshuincho?

Some shrines give a paper goshuin (kakioki) (Miyagi Prefecture Shrine Association and JNTO). Meiji Jingu says a paper goshuin is also available for people without a book. However, how this is handled differs by shrine.

### How much is the hatsuho-ryo?

JNTO gives a guideline of about 300 to 500 yen. At Meiji Jingu it is 500 yen. Special goshuin can cost different amounts.

### Can I use a card or a smartphone payment?

According to Meiji Jingu's official English Q&A, credit cards cannot be used at the goshuin counter. Hokkaido Jingu is also cash only. We have not been able to confirm how other shrines handle this, so it is safest to bring cash.

### May I receive only the goshuin and leave?

We do not recommend it. Shrines guide visitors to pray first. Kifune Jinja says it gives goshuin only to people who have visited in person.

### May I collect goshuin from other shrines and temples in the same book?

Some people keep them separate, but whether to separate them is a matter of personal preference (Japan Tourism Agency multilingual commentary database). Some shrines and temples decline a book that carries seals of another religion, so if you are concerned, ask when you make your request.

### May I take a photo while it is being written?

The Miyagi Prefecture Shrine Association treats peering at the writing or taking photographs without permission as bad manners. If you want to take a photo, ask for permission first.

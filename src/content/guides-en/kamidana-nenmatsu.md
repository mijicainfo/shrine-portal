## Year-End Is the Season to Tidy Your Kamidana

Giving thanks to the o-fuda (sacred paper talisman) you have enshrined for the past year, purifying the kamidana, and welcoming the new year with a fresh o-fuda: this is the traditional way of closing out the year at a home altar. The official website of Jinja Honcho (the Association of Shinto Shrines) explains that, just as households do a big year-end cleaning to prepare for the new year, the kamidana is also cleaned thoroughly, a new o-fuda is enshrined, and the new year is welcomed.

This article walks through how to return old o-fuda and omamori (amulets), how to clean the kamidana, how to change the water for the sakaki (sacred evergreen branches), how to welcome new o-fuda, what to offer at the turn of the year, and how the kamidana is treated in a year when a family member has died. It is based on the guidance of Jinja Honcho, Ise Jingu, the Tokyo and Kyoto shrine associations, and others. For how to choose a kamidana and where to put it, see [Setting Up a Kamidana at Home](/en/guides/kamidana-kazarikata/); for the basics of enshrinement, such as how to arrange your o-fuda, see our [How to Enshrine a Kamidana](/en/kamidana/) page. Please note that practices differ by region and by shrine. When in doubt, asking your ujigami shrine (your local shrine) is the surest way.

## The Year-End Routine at a Glance

Combining the guidance of Jinja Honcho and the Tokyo Shrine Association, the year-end routine looks roughly like this.

1. Prepare to receive new o-fuda (Jingu Taima, the Ise Jingu o-fuda, are made ready at shrines from around October)
2. Clean the kamidana thoroughly
3. Thank the old o-fuda and omamori you have kept for the year, and return them to a shrine
4. Place the new o-fuda in the cleaned miyagata (the altar shrine itself)
5. Welcome the new year

The details of order and timing vary by shrine. For example, the Tokyo Shrine Association explains that the new o-fuda are placed in a miyagata that has been cleaned and left in a pure state.

## How to Return Old O-fuda and Omamori

### When and Where

According to the official Jinja Honcho website, o-fuda are best renewed every year: the o-fuda you enshrined for a year are returned to a shrine at the end of the year and ritually burned (o-takiage). The place to return them is the old-talisman return point (koshinsatsu-osamedokoro) of the shrine where you received them. Omamori are handled the same way: they are returned at year's end and new ones received, although Jinja Honcho also says there is no problem carrying one until your wish is fulfilled.

The official Ise Jingu website likewise says to return o-fuda that have protected you for the year, with gratitude, to your ujigami or chinju shrine. At Jingu's own return points (at the Outer and Inner Shrines), o-fuda and omamori are accepted, while items other than those, such as shimenawa (sacred straw rope) or ornaments, cannot be accepted.

### Items Received Far Away, or at Another Shrine

According to Jinja Honcho, if the shrine where you received the item is too far to visit, it is better to first ask a nearby shrine whether it can burn the item, and then take it there. Sending it to the original shrine with a note of your sentiments is also mentioned as an option. The Tokyo Shrine Association also says that it is desirable to return items to the shrine where they were received, but that items received far away, such as on a trip, may be returned to a nearby shrine even if it is a different shrine.

In practice, though, what is accepted differs from shrine to shrine. For example, the official Meiji Jingu website states that its return point accepts only Meiji Jingu's own o-fuda and omamori, and that engimono (lucky charms) cannot be accepted. The official website of Sapporo Gokoku Shrine states that it also accepts o-fuda, omamori and other items from other shrines for burning, in exchange for a set hatsuho-ryo (offering). Before you go, it is a good idea to check with the shrine you plan to visit.

### Returning Them at Dondo-yaki (Sagicho)

According to Jinja Honcho, at many shrines a fire festival called "sagicho" or "dondo-yaki" is held between New Year's Eve and January 15 (Koshogatsu, "Little New Year"), and old o-fuda and omamori are burned there together with New Year decorations. The Tokyo Shrine Association also explains that, depending on the region, some shrines burn them together with pine decorations and shimenawa at the sagicho or dondo-yaki ritual on January 15.

Dates, reception periods, and the items that can be brought differ by region and shrine. Whether you return yours before the end of the year or after New Year, check the shrine's notice in advance.

### Can Old and New O-fuda Be Enshrined Together?

The Tokyo Shrine Association says there is no problem at all with enshrining them together, but advises swapping in the new o-fuda as much as possible and, when the occasion arises, bringing the old ones to a nearby shrine to be burned. It also says that an o-fuda received during a prayer service (kito) may be kept enshrined until your prayer is fulfilled, after which it is good to visit the shrine to give thanks and return the o-fuda.

## Cleaning the Kamidana

### The December 13 "Susuharai"

The official Jinja Honcho website introduces "susuharai" as an event in which the inside and outside of the house are cleaned once a year. It is still often held on December 13 throughout Japan, and it also carries the meaning of the start of preparations for welcoming the toshigami (the New Year deity). The household procedure it describes is to clean the kamidana first, and then the kitchen and the other rooms. In recent years more people do their big cleaning at the very end of the year, and Jinja Honcho suggests that you might try doing susuharai for the kamidana alone on December 13.

### Keeping It Pure

In the sources we checked, we could not find an official guide that specifies in detail how to wipe the kamidana, or how to handle the o-fuda while cleaning. The underlying idea is to keep the altar pure. The Tokyo Shrine Association advises not to place gifts or souvenirs on the kamidana, and always to keep it pure. The Kyoto Shrine Association asks that o-fuda, being sacred, not be put on the ground but kept in a place where they are treated with respect and not stepped over. If you are unsure about cleaning procedure, ask the priest at your ujigami shrine.

## Sakaki: Changing the Water and Replacing the Branches

Jinja Honcho explains that sakaki are offered on both sides of the kamidana, that sakaki is an evergreen that has long been used for offerings before the kami, and it advises changing the water every day to keep the branches fresh. The Tokyo and Kyoto shrine associations also say to change the water in the sakaki vase every morning.

As for how often to replace the sakaki branches themselves, we could not find a clear statement in the Jinja Honcho or shrine association pages we checked. Miyazaki Shrine in Higashihiroshima, Hiroshima Prefecture, introduces "the 1st and 15th of each month" as a guideline in an article on its official website, and says that it is only a guideline, that the timing varies with the local climate and season, and that you should not be overly rigid but replace them as needed. The same article says changing the water every day is best, but that if that is difficult you can change it every three or five days, or at least once a week, and that when changing the water, removing the scale from the stems and the dead leaves helps keep the branches green.

Following that guideline, December 1 and 15 would be the replacement days within the year. We could not confirm an official rule on when to renew the sakaki at year-end. Checking their condition while you do the big cleaning may be a good idea so that you can welcome the new year comfortably.

## Welcoming New O-fuda

### Jingu Taima and Your Ujigami's O-fuda

The Ise Jingu o-fuda, called Jingu Taima, are distributed through shrines across Japan. According to Jinja Honcho, they are made ready from around October; for details, ask your ujigami or a nearby shrine, and it encourages people to receive the new Jingu Taima by the end of each year and welcome the new year. The official Ise Jingu website also says that they are distributed through shrines nationwide before the New Year, and encourages households, before New Year, to enshrine the new Jingu Taima together with the o-fuda of the ujigami or chinju shrine that protects the area, praying for a safe and happy year for the family. The Shimane Shrine Association, however, describes enshrining the new Jingu Taima at the start of the year, so the wording about when to enshrine new o-fuda differs somewhat from one source to another.

The Tokyo Shrine Association explains that replacing the o-fuda of Ise and the ujigami with new ones when welcoming the New Year carries the meaning of receiving newer power and newer life.

### The Order of Placement

According to Jinja Honcho, in a three-door (sansha-zukuri) miyagata, the Jingu Taima goes in the center, the ujigami's o-fuda to the right as you face it, and the o-fuda of a shrine you particularly revere to the left. In a single-door (issha-zukuri) miyagata, they are stacked from the front: Jingu Taima, the ujigami's o-fuda, then the o-fuda of the shrine you revere. When there are too many o-fuda to fit in the miyagata, they are carefully lined up beside it.

If you moved during the year, according to the Jinja Honcho Q&A, you receive an o-fuda from your new ujigami and enshrine it, keep the previous ujigami's o-fuda alongside it until the end of that year, and have it burned at the new ujigami shrine.

## Year-End and New Year Offerings, and the Shimenawa

According to Jinja Honcho, the basic offerings at a kamidana are rice, salt, and water, with sake as well if possible. Seasonal first harvests or sweets you have received are also fine. The Tokyo Shrine Association says that on the 1st and 15th of the month, or on family celebrations, sake, fish, vegetables, and fruit may also be offered with extra care. The Kyoto Shrine Association likewise says that on New Year's and on the 1st of each month, sake, vegetables, and fruit are offered in addition to rice, salt, and water. The Tokyo Shrine Association also suggests offering seasonal first harvests and souvenirs with gratitude when welcoming the New Year.

As for the shimenawa, Jinja Honcho explains that it marks a sacred, pure place, that shide (zigzag paper streamers) are tucked into it, and that it is better, if possible, to prepare one for a home kamidana as well. According to the Kyoto Shrine Association, when hanging a daikon-jime (a shimenawa that tapers like a radish), it is common to hang it with the thicker end on the right as you face the kamidana. In the official pages we checked, we could not find anything on when to replace the shimenawa and New Year decorations at year-end, or on how to display kagami-mochi (mirror rice cakes). Please follow local custom and the guidance of your ujigami shrine.

## When There Has Been a Death in the Family

When a family member dies, there is a custom of temporarily refraining from worship at the kamidana. According to Jinja Honcho's page on "bukki" (mourning), during the "ki" period, visiting shrines and home worship (worship at the kamidana) are also refrained from, and in many cases a white sheet of hanshi paper is pasted on the front of the kamidana, or a folding screen is set up, to suspend the worship temporarily. This period varies by region but is generally 50 days, up to the fiftieth-day ritual (gojunichisai), and once the 50 days of "ki" have passed, resuming home worship is in principle considered acceptable. Where a region has its own custom, following it is appropriate. The Tokyo, Kyoto and Aichi shrine associations also describe covering the front of the kamidana with white paper, and treating about fifty days as the guideline. The Aichi Shrine Association describes a flow in which, after the fiftieth-day ritual, the household is purified (kiyoharai) and worship at the kamidana resumes. This custom is sometimes called "kamidana-fuji" (sealing the kamidana).

For when this overlaps with the year-end and New Year, Jinja Honcho says that although some people hesitate to enshrine a new o-fuda in the new year after a death in the family, renewing the o-fuda every year is the essential meaning of the kamidana, that it is good to receive and enshrine a new o-fuda once the "ki" period has passed, and that when the year-end and New Year overlap with the "ki" period, you should receive the o-fuda after the period ends. Separately from "ki," Jinja Honcho says the "buku" period of mourning is generally up to the first-year memorial (isshuki). The Tokyo Shrine Association explains, however, that "mochu" and "kichu" originally have different meanings but are sometimes now treated as synonyms, so the terms are used in various ways. For the specific period and how to proceed, please consult your ujigami shrine.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q. When should I return my o-fuda?

Jinja Honcho says to return them to a shrine at the end of the year to be burned. Many shrines also accept old o-fuda at dondo-yaki and similar events between New Year's Eve and January 15, but timing and reception methods differ by shrine.

### Q. What if I cannot go to the shrine where I received it?

Jinja Honcho introduces two options: asking a nearby shrine whether it can burn the item before bringing it, or sending it to the shrine where you received it with a note of your sentiments.

### Q. Can I return o-fuda from other shrines?

The Tokyo Shrine Association says items received far away may be returned to a nearby shrine. Some shrines accept only their own items, so we recommend checking in advance.

### Q. What should I do with an o-fuda I received at a prayer service?

According to the Tokyo Shrine Association, you may keep it enshrined until your prayer is fulfilled. Once it is, it is good to visit the shrine to give thanks and return the o-fuda.

### Q. Do I have to replace my o-fuda every year?

Jinja Honcho says it is good to receive a new o-fuda each year. It explains that, just as the new year makes us feel renewed, a new o-fuda means the kami protects us with fresh power.

### Q. What about new o-fuda if a family member died during the year?

Jinja Honcho advises receiving the o-fuda after the "ki" period has ended when it overlaps with the year-end and New Year. Please consult your ujigami shrine for details.

### Q. Can I enshrine a new o-fuda in a home without a kamidana?

The Tokyo Shrine Association introduces placing a miyagata on a cleaned chest of drawers or bookshelf, and also mentions small miyagata that can be hung on a wall.

## In Summary

The basic flow of year-end kamidana care is to return your old o-fuda with thanks for the year, purify the altar, and welcome the new o-fuda. The timing of returns, dondo-yaki reception, how to change sakaki, and what to offer all vary by region and shrine. Using the official guidance as a reference, what matters most is to keep going sincerely, at a pace you can manage.

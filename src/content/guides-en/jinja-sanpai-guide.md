## The Flow of a Shrine Visit (for First-Timers)

A shrine visit can be divided into six steps. There are no difficult rules. If you remember the flow of **"bow at the entrance," "purify your hands," "pray with bows and claps," and "bow again on the way out,"** you will be fine.

<figcaption class="mt-2 text-xs leading-relaxed text-shrine-ink-soft">The shrine visit in six steps<br />Illustration: created by this site</figcaption>
</figure>

1. Bow in front of the torii gate, then pass through
2. Walk along the sando (approach path), avoiding the center
3. Purify your hands and mouth at the temizuya (water basin)
4. At the haiden (worship hall), make an offering and pray
5. When you have finished praying, step back quietly
6. At the torii on your way out, turn around and bow again

Fine points of etiquette differ from shrine to shrine. In that case, **please follow the signs and guidance of that shrine**.

## 1. Passing Through the Torii

A torii is the boundary between the realm of the kami and the everyday world. The Japan National Tourism Organization (JNTO) explains that the proper etiquette is to bow once in front of the torii before entering.

The Japan Tourism Agency's multilingual commentary database (the commentary on Shirayama Hime Jinja) also explains that the torii is the entrance to a shrine and a passage from the secular world to a sacred place, and that you show respect by bowing once before passing through.

## 2. Walking the Sando

The path that leads from the torii toward the main hall is the sando. The Japan Tourism Agency database (Shirayama Hime Jinja) advises walking toward the edge, because the center of the sando is said to be the path of the kami. JNTO also explains that it is the custom not to pass through the middle of a torii head-on, but to go through slightly to the left or right.

Ise Jingu asks visitors to walk on the left side at the Geku (Outer Shrine) and on the right side at the Naiku (Inner Shrine). In this way, some shrines have their own rules.

## 3. Purifying Your Hands and Mouth at the Temizuya

At the "temizuya" (also read "chozuya"), the water basin along the sando, you purify your hands and mouth before praying. Ise Jingu explains that washing your hands and mouth is a simplified form of misogi, a purification rite.

<figure class="not-prose my-8">
<img src="/images/guides/sanpai-guide/temizuya.webp" alt="A temizuya with ladles laid out" width="1000" height="750" loading="lazy" decoding="async" class="w-full rounded-2xl border border-shrine-gold/25 shadow-sm" />
<figcaption class="mt-2 text-xs leading-relaxed text-shrine-ink-soft">A temizuya with ladles (Kasuga Shrine, Nerima, Tokyo)<br />Photo: <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Kasuga-jinja_purification_basin_2023-08-03.jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" class="underline">Asanagi</a> / CC0 (Wikimedia Commons)</figcaption>
</figure>

Ise Jingu officially gives the following five steps.

<figure class="not-prose my-8">
<img src="/images/guides/sanpai-guide/temizu.svg" alt="Illustration of the temizu purification in five pictograms" width="960" height="250" loading="lazy" decoding="async" class="w-full rounded-2xl border border-shrine-gold/25" />
<figcaption class="mt-2 text-xs leading-relaxed text-shrine-ink-soft">The five steps of temizu (based on the guidance of Ise Jingu)<br />Illustration: created by this site</figcaption>
</figure>

1. Take a generous scoop of water with the ladle in your right hand and wash your left hand.
2. Switch the ladle to your left hand and wash your right hand.
3. Take the ladle in your right hand again, pour water into your left hand and rinse your mouth. **Do not put the ladle directly to your mouth.**
4. Wash your left hand once more.
5. Use the remaining water to rinse the handle of the ladle, then put it back.

Kitano Tenmangu gives almost the same steps, and adds that you must not put the ladle directly to your mouth or drink the water. Meiji Jingu gives a simpler form of the flow: "wash both hands," "pour water into both hands and rinse your mouth," and "wash both hands again." When in doubt, follow the guidance of that shrine.

## 4. Praying at the Haiden

In front of the haiden (worship hall), you make an offering and then pray. The basic form is "nihai nihakushu ippai (bow twice, clap twice, bow once)": two deep bows, two claps, and finally one deep bow.

<figure class="not-prose my-8">
<img src="/images/guides/sanpai-guide/bow-clap.svg" alt="Illustration of bowing twice, clapping twice and bowing once" width="960" height="250" loading="lazy" decoding="async" class="w-full rounded-2xl border border-shrine-gold/25" />
<figcaption class="mt-2 text-xs leading-relaxed text-shrine-ink-soft">Bow twice, clap twice, bow once: 1-2 bow deeply twice, 3-4 clap twice, 5 bow deeply once<br />Illustration: created by this site</figcaption>
</figure>

The steps given by Ise Jingu are as follows.

1. Step before the sanctuary and straighten your posture.
2. With a flat back, bend at the waist to 90 degrees and bow deeply twice.
3. Bring your hands together at chest height, with your right fingertips slightly lower.
4. Open your hands to about shoulder width and clap twice.
5. Bring your fingertips back together and, finally, bow deeply once more.

Meiji Jingu gives this guidance: after making an offering, bow twice, clap twice (praying at the center of your joined hands), and finally bow deeply once. Kitano Tenmangu gives the same flow.

### Examples where the number of claps differs by shrine

The official Q&A of Izumo Taisha says that the common form is "nirei nihakushu ichirei (bow twice, clap twice, bow once)," but that Izumo Taisha's formal worship is "nirei yonhakushu ichirei (bow twice, clap four times, bow once)," and that it is the same at the sanctuaries other than the main hall. Only at the annual grand festival (May 14) is it eight claps. It also says that the sincerity of your prayer is no different whatever the number of claps. The Japan Tourism Agency database also includes a commentary that at the Upper Shrine of Usa Jingu, you clap four times instead of twice.

When in doubt, **following the signs and guidance of that shrine** is the surest way.

## 5. Bow Again at the Torii on Your Way Out

The Japan Tourism Agency database (Shirayama Hime Jinja) advises that after you pass out through the torii on your way home, you turn around and bow once more to show respect.

## The Difference Between Shrines and Temples

Japan has both shrines and temples. What they enshrine and their etiquette are different. JNTO explains as follows.

- **Entrance:** The entrance of a shrine is a torii; the entrance of a temple is a sanmon (temple gate).
- **Religion:** A shrine is Shinto; a temple is Buddhist.
- **Etiquette for praying:** At a shrine, you clap your hands. At a temple, you do not clap (you quietly put your palms together).
- **Incense:** At many temples, you put in an offering, take incense sticks, and put out the flame by fanning it with your hand (not by blowing on it).

## Etiquette in the Grounds

- **Clothing:** Meiji Jingu asks visitors to dress in a way suited to visiting a shrine and to spend their time quietly. It also notes that the grounds are a forest with insects, so you should keep exposed skin to a minimum.
- **Photography:** Ise Jingu says that there are places where photography is prohibited, and prohibits long photography sessions with a tripod, stepladders, drones, live streaming, commercial photography and the like. Kitano Tenmangu asks visitors to refrain from taking group photos in busy places.
- **Eating, drinking and smoking:** Ise Jingu says smoking is not allowed within the sacred grounds, and asks visitors to refrain from eating and drinking there and to do so at the rest areas.
- **Pets:** Ise Jingu says that you cannot visit with pets (they are looked after at the entrance). Izumo Taisha says that pets cannot accompany you inside buildings. This differs by shrine, so check in advance.

- **Omamori (amulets):** Meiji Jingu explains that an omamori is something you keep close to you.
- **Ema (votive plaques):** Meiji Jingu asks visitors to write their wishes, thanks or vows and dedicate the plaque.
- **Old amulets and ofuda (talismans):** Meiji Jingu asks visitors to return them to the "koshinpu osamedokoro" (old-talisman collection point) in the grounds (on the right side as you face the south temizuya). Kitano Tenmangu explains that old amulets and ofuda are sacred things that carry the spirit of Tenjin-sama, so you should not treat them carelessly and should return them to the collection point. As a rule, you return them to the shrine where you received them.
- **Goshuin:** These are received as proof of your visit. For details, please see our separate feature article, "[Goshuin for Beginners](/en/guides/goshuin-nyumon/)."

## For Visitors from Overseas

- **Prepare cash (Japanese yen).** Some places, such as the offering counters, do not accept cards (for example, the goshuin counter at Meiji Jingu).
- **Check the reception hours in advance.** The offering counters can close earlier than the hours when you can enter the grounds.
- **Multilingual guidance is available.** The Japan Tourism Agency's multilingual commentary database carries explanations of shrine etiquette in Traditional Chinese, English and other languages. JNTO's guidance also has explanations of etiquette at Japanese shrines and temples.
- **If you are not sure, it is fine to watch the people around you or to ask at the shamusho (shrine office).**

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Are there set hours for visiting?

They differ by shrine. For example, Ise Jingu accepts visitors from 5:00 AM, and its closing time changes with the season. Kitano Tenmangu gives its gate opening as 7:00 AM and its offering counter hours as 9:00 AM to 4:30 PM (these may change depending on the time of year). Please check the official website of each shrine before you go.

### How much should I put in as an offering?

According to the Japan Tourism Agency database (the commentary on Meiji Jingu), there is no recommended amount for the offering. What matters is that you pray with sincerity.

### Is it rude if I get the etiquette wrong?

Fine points of etiquette can differ by shrine. Some shrines, like Izumo Taisha, use a different number of claps. What matters is that you pray with respect and gratitude. When in doubt, follow the guidance of that shrine.

### May I take photos at a shrine?

Photography is possible in some places, but there are also places where it is prohibited and ways of taking photos that shrines ask you to avoid. As in the guidance of Ise Jingu and Kitano Tenmangu, check the rules of each shrine and take care not to bother other visitors.

### When and where can I receive a goshuin?

After you have prayed, it is received at the offering counter (shamusho). Hours and locations differ by shrine. For details, please see "[Goshuin for Beginners](/en/guides/goshuin-nyumon/)."

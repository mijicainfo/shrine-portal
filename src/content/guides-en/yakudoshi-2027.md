## Yakudoshi chart for 2027 (Reiwa 9)

Yakudoshi are counted by **kazoedoshi**, the traditional way of counting age: you are 1 at birth, and you gain one year every New Year. For 2027, kazoedoshi age is "2027 minus birth year plus 1." The chart below applies this to birth years.

According to the Tokyo Shrine Association and Meiji Jingu, the yakudoshi are generally, in kazoedoshi, **ages 25, 42 and 61 for men and ages 19, 33 and 37 for women**. Age 42 for men and age 33 for women are called "taiyaku" (the major unlucky year). The years before and after the main year are called "mae-yaku" and "ato-yaku," and together these three years are said to call for special care.

### What is kazoedoshi (traditional Japanese age)?

Kazoedoshi is a traditional way of counting age that has been used in Japan since long ago. You are 1 in the year you are born, and you gain one year **every time New Year's Day (January 1) arrives**, not on your birthday.

The age you usually use (international age) starts at 0 at birth and goes up on each birthday, so it differs from kazoedoshi by one or two years. The Meiji Jingu website explains kazoedoshi as your international age plus 2 before your birthday this year, or plus 1 after your birthday.

You can also work it out from your birth year. Kazoedoshi in 2027 is "2027 minus your birth year plus 1." For example, someone born in 1990 is 2027 − 1990 + 1 = 38 (their international age in 2027 is 36 before their birthday and 37 after it).

The tables in this article were made from kazoedoshi ages converted to birth years. You do not need to calculate your age: **just find the row for your birth year** to see your mae-yaku, hon-yaku and ato-yaku.

## When does an unlucky year start? How kazoedoshi works

Kitano Tenmangu's website explains that yakudoshi are based on kazoedoshi, and that you enter the unlucky year when the new year begins, regardless of your birthday or whether you were born early in the year. In other words, from January 1, 2027, you count that year as your yakudoshi even if your birthday has not yet come.

Meiji Jingu explains kazoedoshi as your age in years plus 2 before your birthday, or plus 1 after your birthday.

## What is yakudoshi?

The Tokyo Shrine Association explains that yakudoshi are the ages in a person's life at which one is likely to meet some kind of misfortune. It says that even today, with advanced medicine, people are still aware of them as ages at which to be careful in everything.

The Association describes yakudoshi as turning points in life that still apply to modern life, ages at which it is easy to lose balance physically and mentally. Kitano Tenmangu also says that yakudoshi have long been regarded as milestone years in life, when one's fortune dips and one tends to fall ill.

Tsurugaoka Hachimangu explains that yakudoshi are said to be times when one is prone to disasters or illness, but also important turning points in life, and so years to spend carefully.

## When should you go for yakubarai?

As a traditional guideline, Tsurugaoka Hachimangu and Kitano Tenmangu point to Setsubun.

- **Kitano Tenmangu**: Yakudoshi are based on kazoedoshi, and you enter the unlucky year when the new year begins, so it recommends visiting as early as possible, from the start of the year until Setsubun.
- **Tsurugaoka Hachimangu**: It says that since ancient times, Japanese people have made it a custom to receive a yakuyoke prayer by Setsubun, the seasonal turning point, to clear away bad fortune. The shrine holds the "Tsurugaoka Yakuyoke Taisai" in a period in late January that includes a weekend; in Reiwa 8 it ran from January 30 to February 1. It also invites whole families to visit, not only those in a yakudoshi.
- **Meiji Jingu**: It says it has long been said to be during the New Year's matsu-no-uchi or around Setsubun, but that these days many people receive the purification throughout the year, and it accepts yakubarai prayers every day at the Kaguraden.

Setsubun in 2027 falls on February 3. The period from the start of the year to Setsubun overlaps with hatsumode and yakuyoke season, and some shrines become crowded. Check the official website of the shrine you plan to visit for reception hours and crowding.

## The yakubarai (yakuyoke prayer): procedure, fee and clothing

The Tokyo Shrine Association explains that the usual way of yakubarai is to visit a shrine and receive a prayer. Here is the procedure at Kitano Tenmangu, as described on its official website.

- **Reservation and reception**: No reservation is needed. Reception is from 9:00 AM to 4:00 PM, and visitors are guided in order after reception. Large group prayers need a reservation in advance.
- **Prayer fee**: 5,000 yen (talisman and offerings), 8,000 yen (talisman, offerings and a "Daifuku Koume" item), 10,000 yen (talisman, offerings, plum liqueur and a "Daifuku Koume" item). Special prayers of 10,000 yen or more are also accepted (2026 information).
- **Duration**: The prayer takes about 20 minutes, and can take longer in busy periods.
- **Clothing**: Clothing for visiting the shrine is basically free. But the prayer is a solemn ritual in which you go up into the worship hall, express your daily gratitude before the deity, and deliver your wishes. Kitano Tenmangu asks visitors to avoid casual clothing such as T-shirts, jeans and shorts, and says suits with a tie for men and similarly formal clothing for women are desirable.
- **Photography**: Visitors are asked to refrain from taking photographs inside the main hall, during the prayer and also before and after it.

Fees and reception differ by shrine, so check the official website of the shrine you plan to visit. Some shrines also hold prayers for people who cannot visit, for example because they live far away (Kitano Tenmangu asks visitors to contact it by phone).

## After your yakudoshi: "Oreimairi" (a thank-you visit)

The Kitano Tenmangu website explains that it is an old custom to make an "oreimairi," a visit to express gratitude to the deity, when a wish has been fulfilled or when you have passed your unlucky year safely after a yakuyoke prayer. It asks visitors to return any amulets received at the time of the prayer to the old-talisman collection point.

For an oreimairi, you apply at the prayer reception at the offering counter in front of the main hall, write your name, address and purpose, and give an offering as you see fit. A thanksgiving ritual is held in the main hall, and you receive the offerings (Kitano Tenmangu).

## If you are not in a yakudoshi but want a purification

For people who are not in a yakudoshi but have had a run of bad luck lately, Meiji Jingu says that its Kaguraden hall accepts prayers for removing misfortune and inviting good fortune every day. It explains that, since ancient times, people in Japan have believed that purifying away misfortune brings about the removal of disaster and the arrival of good fortune.

## Frequently asked questions

### When do yakudoshi start and end?

Counted in kazoedoshi, you enter that year's yakudoshi when the new year begins (January 1). Your birthday and whether you were born early in the year make no difference (Kitano Tenmangu). Together, mae-yaku, hon-yaku and ato-yaku make three years.

### Must yakubarai be done at a shrine?

The Tokyo Shrine Association explains that the usual way is to visit a shrine and receive a prayer. Reception and fees differ by shrine, so check the shrine you plan to visit.

### Can my family go with me if I am in a yakudoshi?

Tsurugaoka Hachimangu invites families to visit together, whether or not they are in a yakudoshi (Tsurugaoka Yakuyoke Taisai). Check with other shrines about how they handle it.

### How much is the fee?

It differs by shrine. Kitano Tenmangu's 2026 information starts at 5,000 yen. Check the reception details on the official website of the shrine you plan to visit.

### What if I cannot visit in person?

Some shrines hold prayers for people who cannot visit, for example because they live far away. Kitano Tenmangu asks visitors to contact it by phone.

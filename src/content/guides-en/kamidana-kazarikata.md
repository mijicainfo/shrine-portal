## Welcoming a Kamidana into Your Home

A small altar for enshrining, with sincerity, the o-fuda (sacred paper talisman) you received at a shrine — rather than simply leaving it on a shelf. This is what is called a "kamidana" (home Shinto altar). Having a kamidana at home naturally creates a habit of pressing your hands together in prayer morning and evening, and lets you feel closer, in your everyday life, to your connection with your ujigami (local guardian deity) or a shrine you hold dear.

This article walks step by step through how to choose a miyagata (the altar shrine itself), where to place it, how to arrange the altar fittings, and the daily worship etiquette — for anyone newly welcoming a kamidana into their home. We also introduce altar fittings that are actually easy to get hold of, as a reference for choosing your own. For a more detailed explanation of how to arrange your o-fuda and where to place them, please also see our [How to Enshrine a Kamidana](/en/kamidana/) page.

## Choosing a Miyagata (Altar Shrine)

There are several types of miyagata, differing in the number of doors. If you have only a single o-fuda, or if this is your first time enshrining a kamidana, a simple "issha-zukuri" (single-door style) with one door is often chosen. If you would like to enshrine multiple o-fuda side by side, choosing a "sansha-zukuri" (three-door style) with three doors lined up makes the relative rank of each o-fuda visually easy to understand.

## Where to Place Your Kamidana

The basic rule is to place the kamidana in a bright, clean room where the family gathers, such as the living room, positioned higher than eye level, and facing south or east. Avoid placing it directly above a frequently used door, or near a toilet or washroom. For more detailed placement notes and illustrations, see our [How to Enshrine a Kamidana](/en/kamidana/) page.

## Assembling Altar Fittings for a Beautiful Enshrinement

The basic offerings for a kamidana are rice, salt, and water, ideally replaced with fresh ones every day. The set of vessels for presenting these offerings is called "shingu" (altar fittings). Choosing a set that includes a sakaki-tate (sacred sakaki branch stand), heishi (sake vessels), mizutama (water vessel), and plates lets even first-timers gather a complete set all at once.

## Setting a Sacred Space with a Shimenawa

Hanging a shimenawa (sacred straw rope) in front of the kamidana serves as a "kekkai," marking that space as sacred. It is not essential, but displaying one alongside the miyagata and altar fittings gives the whole altar a more complete, well-arranged feel.

## Daily Worship Etiquette

Each morning, after refreshing the offerings, stand in front of the kamidana and follow the "two bows, two claps, one bow" ritual, greeting the start of the day and expressing your everyday gratitude. What matters most is not getting the etiquette exactly right, but the sincere feeling of pressing your hands together in prayer to the kami each day. For a detailed look at the types of offerings and how often to replace your o-fuda, see our [How to Enshrine a Kamidana](/en/kamidana/) page.

## In Summary

A kamidana is never a large or elaborate installation — with just a miyagata and the basic altar fittings, any household can begin enshrining one without difficulty. It may be a good idea to start with a single-door miyagata and a minimal set of altar fittings, and gradually build things up to suit your lifestyle. For more on the meaning and types of o-fuda themselves, please also see our [Guide to Shrine Amulets and Talismans](/en/items/).

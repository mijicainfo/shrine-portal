## History

Oyama Jinja is the great shrine of Mount Tateyama worship, venerating Mount Oyama (3,003 meters), the highest peak of the Tateyama range, as its object of worship. According to shrine tradition, in 701 (Taiho 1) a man named Saeki no Ariyori was led into Tateyama's sacred heights by a white falcon and a bear, and there opened the mountain to religious practice. Ever since, Tateyama has been revered as one of Japan's three great sacred mountains alongside Mount Fuji and Mount Haku, and Oyama Jinja has taken shape as three linked sites: the Mine-honsha (peak main shrine) at the summit of Mount Oyama itself, the Chugu Kigan-den (middle shrine prayer hall) at Ashikuraji on the mountain's flank, and this Maetate-shadan (front shrine altar), down on the plains at Iwakuraji.

Because the Mine-honsha sits in the harsh environment of the summit, it can only be visited during the short summer climbing season. For that reason, this Maetate-shadan — set on a river terrace where the Jokanji River fans out onto the plain — has long functioned as the shrine's effective sato-miya, the site most visitors actually reach. Its deities are Izanagi no Kami (known here as Tateyama Daigongen or Oyama no Kami, whose honji-suijaku Buddhist form is Amida Nyorai) and Amenotajikarao no Kami (known as Tao Tenjin or Tsurugidake no Kami, whose honji form is Fudo Myoo), preserving the syncretic Shinto-Buddhist character that long defined Tateyama worship.

The present honden is believed to date from the Muromachi period and, as the largest surviving shrine main hall in the Hokuriku region, was designated a National Important Cultural Property in 1906. Historical figures including Minamoto no Yoritomo, Ashikaga Yoshitane, and Sassa Narimasa are all said to have venerated the shrine, a testament to the patronage of generations of warlords and rulers who helped maintain its buildings over the centuries.

## Highlights

The single greatest highlight of the Maetate-shadan is its honden, built in the five-bay nagare-zukuri style with a cypress-bark roof in the Muromachi tradition. Among surviving shrine main halls in the Hokuriku region, it is one of the largest, its interior finished in red and black lacquer with gilt fittings in a solemn, dignified style. Passing through the Omote-shinmon gate, the sheer weight of history is palpable in the honden standing beyond the haiden.

The grounds themselves occupy a quiet spot on the bank of the Jokanji River, the sound of running water mingling with the greenery of the torii and sacred grove for a calm, contemplative atmosphere. Visiting the Mine-honsha and Chugu Kigan-den as well gives a fuller sense of how Tateyama worship spans from the summit all the way down to the plain.

## Visiting Tips

Visiting the Maetate-shadan alone takes about 20–30 minutes, but since goshuin stamps can be received separately at the Mine-honsha, the Chugu Kigan-den, and the Maetate-shadan, visitors wanting the fuller picture of Tateyama worship should also plan to visit the Chugu Kigan-den at Ashikuraji and the Tateyama Museum, both about 20 minutes away by car. Reaching the Mine-honsha at the summit requires proper mountaineering gear and is only possible during the summer climbing season (roughly early July to early September) — a very different undertaking from visiting the Maetate-shadan or Chugu Kigan-den.

## Annual Festivals

- **Saitan-sai (January 1), Nikku-hajime-sai / Traffic Safety Prayer Rite (January 2), and Gensi-sai (January 3)**: New Year rites celebrating the year's arrival and praying for safety and prosperity ahead.
- **Autumn Grand Festival (November 3)**: The Maetate-shadan's most important festival, featuring a dance designated as an intangible cultural property of the town.
- **Oharai and Omisoka Rites (December 31, 4:00 PM and 11:00 PM)**: Purification rites closing out the year and welcoming in the new one.
- **Monthly Festival and Traffic Safety Prayer Rite (1st of every month)**: A recurring monthly rite praying for the safety and peace of the local community.

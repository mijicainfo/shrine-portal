## History

The ground on which Minatogawa Jinja stands is itself a historic battlefield — the site where Kusunoki Masashige and his clansmen met a dramatic end in 1336, during the Nanboku-cho period of civil war between the Northern and Southern Imperial Courts. Acting on the orders of Emperor Go-Daigo, Masashige had fought to topple the Kamakura shogunate, and later took up arms again against Ashikaga Takauji, who was moving to establish a rival warrior government. Vastly outnumbered, Masashige's forces were defeated at Minatogawa after a fierce battle, and he is said to have taken his own life there alongside his younger brother Masasue and fourteen other clansmen, the brothers vowing to be "reborn seven times to destroy the enemies of the court." According to tradition, on his way to Minatogawa, Masashige stopped at Sakurai (in present-day Shimamoto, Osaka Prefecture) to deliver a famous set of final instructions to his young son Masatsura before sending him home to Kawachi — an episode remembered ever after as the "Parting at Sakurai."

Masashige's grave was quietly tended for centuries after his death, until 1692, when Tokugawa Mitsukuni — the lord of the Mito domain, popularly known as "Mito Komon" — had the site restored and erected a stone monument in his own hand, inscribed "Ah, the Grave of the Loyal Retainer Kusunoki." Resting on a tortoise-shaped pedestal (kifu), the monument became widely known thereafter as the "Grave of the Great Lord Kusunoki," and is said to have drawn numerous visitors in the final years of the shogunate — including figures such as Sakamoto Ryoma and Kondo Isami — who came to pay their respects to Masashige's loyalty. The monument still stands on the shrine grounds today and has been designated a National Historic Site.

With the Meiji Restoration, Kusunoki Masashige was held up as the supreme model of loyalty to the imperial throne, and in 1868 Emperor Meiji ordered that a shrine be built in his honor. Minatogawa Jinja was formally established on May 24, 1872, on the very spot where Masashige had died, and became the first shrine ever ranked as a bekkaku kanpeisha — a special category of imperially-endowed shrine created specifically to honor such figures of loyal service. More than 150 years since its founding, the shrine is still known with deep affection by its local nickname, "Nanko-san," and remains one of Kobe's most revered shrines.

## Highlights

The single most historically significant site within the shrine grounds is the "Ah, the Grave of the Loyal Retainer Kusunoki" monument, erected by Tokugawa Mitsukuni in 1692. A grand stone stele rests atop a pedestal carved in the shape of the mythical tortoise-like creature kifu, its inscription written in Mitsukuni's own hand. Both this monument and the nearby site traditionally identified as the spot where Masashige and his clan took their own lives have been designated National Historic Sites — a reminder that Minatogawa Jinja is not merely a modern foundation, but stands on the actual ground where its story took place.

The main hall is decorated throughout with the kikusui (chrysanthemum-and-water) crest of the Kusunoki family, and the grounds include several subsidiary shrines: Kannabi Shrine, dedicated to Masashige's wife, and Kusunoki Inari Shrine, known for blessings of business prosperity, among others. A hall dedicated to Bishamonten, one of Kobe's Seven Lucky Gods, also stands within the grounds, making the shrine a stop on Kobe's Seven Lucky Gods pilgrimage route as well as a destination for those tracing Masashige's story.

The grounds are further home to a Noh theater hall, Shinnoden, and a treasure house holding items of armor and sutras designated as Important Cultural Properties. Despite standing within easy walking distance of JR Kobe Station and Kosoku-Kobe Station, right in the heart of the city, the shrine's leafy grounds retain a genuine sense of calm, and it remains one of Kobe's most beloved power spots.

## Visiting Tips

Visiting hours run 9:00–17:00. One of the shrine's great advantages is its location — just 3 minutes from JR Kobe Station and immediately outside Kosoku-Kobe Station — making it an easy addition to a day of sightseeing in central Kobe.

After paying respects at the worship hall, make your way to the "Ah, the Grave of the Loyal Retainer Kusunoki" monument and the traditional death site toward the back of the grounds. Standing before a monument raised by Tokugawa Mitsukuni himself, at a site said to have drawn Sakamoto Ryoma and other figures of the late shogunate, brings a weight to the visit that goes beyond ordinary sightseeing. Goshuin are available at the shrine office between 9:00 and 17:00, with several varieties on offer beyond the main shrine's own, so it's worth bringing a goshuin book along. Roughly a million visitors come through during the first three days of the New Year, making it one of Hyogo Prefecture's busiest hatsumode destinations — worth keeping in mind if you'd prefer to avoid the crowds.

## Annual Festivals

- **Nanko Festival (Nanko-sai; eve festival on May 24, main festival on May 25, closing festival on May 26)**: A lively festival organized by parishioners to commemorate the anniversary of Masashige's death at Minatogawa on the 25th day of the fifth lunar month in 1336. Costumed samurai processions and other celebratory events mark the occasion, making it a beloved early-summer tradition in Kobe.
- **Reisai, the Shrine's Official Annual Festival (July 12)**: The date corresponding, on the modern solar calendar, to the 25th day of the fifth lunar month that gave rise to the Nanko Festival — this is the shrine's formally designated annual festival day, marked with solemn rites performed by the shrine's priests.
- **Summer Festival (late July–August)**: A seasonal festival featuring night stalls and dedication ceremonies, well-loved by the local community.
- **New Year's Visit (January)**: Thanks to its location so close to Kobe Station, the shrine draws roughly a million visitors during the first three days of the New Year, making it one of Hyogo Prefecture's busiest hatsumode destinations.

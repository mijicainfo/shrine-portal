## History

Tatsuta Taisha's founding is traced, in shrine tradition, to the reign of the 10th emperor, Sujin. According to this account, the land suffered years of poor harvests and epidemic disease, and when the emperor prayed for guidance, the deities appeared to him in a dream, instructing that they be enshrined at "Tatsuno field in Tatsuta, where the morning sun rises and the evening sun sets." Once the shrine was built as instructed, the harvests are said to have grown abundant again and the epidemic to have subsided — the founding legend behind the shrine.

The earliest firmly documented reference to worship at Tatsuta appears in the Nihon Shoki, in its entry for the 10th day of the 4th month of 675 CE (the 4th year of Emperor Tenmu's reign), which records that the Imperial court dispatched envoys to worship the wind deity at Tatsuta and the water deity — known as the Great Imibe deity — at Hirose (present-day Hirose Taisha in Kawai, Nara Prefecture) on the same day. From then on, twice each year, in the 4th and 7th months, an imperial envoy of fifth rank or higher together with an official of the Department of Divinities of sixth rank or higher were sent from the court to conduct the Wind God Festival (Fujin-sai) at Tatsuta and the Great Imibe Festival (Oimi-sai) at Hirose. This paired system of wind and water rites continued without interruption until the Nanboku-cho period, and the Heian-era Engishiki already records these observances fixed on the 4th day of the 4th and 7th months. As shrines dedicated to wind and water respectively — the two forces underpinning the land and its harvests — Tatsuta and Hirose held a special place in the state ritual calendar of ancient Japan.

In terms of shrine rank, Tatsuta Taisha was listed as a Myojin Taisha (two-seat) in the Engishiki's register of deities, and was also counted among the "Middle Seven" of the Twenty-Two Shrines especially honored by the Heian court. From the Meiji era it was ranked a Kanpei Taisha, a first-rank government-supported shrine, from 1871 to 1946, and today holds Beppyo status within the Association of Shinto Shrines. The enshrined deities are Ame no Mihashira no Okami (also known as Shinatsuhiko no Kami) and Kuni no Mihashira no Okami (also known as Shinatsuhime no Kami), a male-and-female pair of wind deities also known by the names Shinatsuhiko no Mikoto and Shinatsuhime no Mikoto.

## Highlights

The shrine grounds sit on a rise at Tatono, at the southern foot of the Ikoma mountains overlooking the Yamato River, long known as the seat of the "wind god of Tatsuta." The worship hall and main sanctuary preserve a venerable atmosphere, and the grounds include subsidiary shrines such as Tatsuta Ebisu Jinja, associated with business prosperity, and Shiraryu Jinja (White Dragon Shrine), known for its distinctive practice of pouring water while praying — drawing visitors seeking good relationships and protection from misfortune alike.

Tatsuta Taisha is also known as a popular spot for autumn foliage, with the grounds turning brilliant colors each fall and drawing visitors and photographers alike. Behind the shrine, a mountain trail connects to the Tatsuta Kodo, an ancient road that in the Asuka and Nara periods served as a key route linking the Yamato basin with Naniwa (Osaka), where travelers prayed for safe passage. This historical association is part of why visitors today travel from across Japan to pray for maritime safety, aviation safety, and safe travel more broadly.

## Visiting Tips

The shrine office is open 9:00–17:00, and prayer requests (gokito) are accepted until 16:00. Free parking near the first torii gate makes visiting by car convenient. It's about a 5–10 minute walk from JR Sango Station, though the approach is a gentle uphill slope, so comfortable footwear is recommended. Many visitors combine a visit here with nearby Horyuji Temple in neighboring Ikaruga, or with Shigisan Chogosonshiji Temple.

## Annual Festivals

- **Fuchin Taisai (Wind-Calming Grand Festival, typically the first Sunday of July)**: The shrine's largest festival, carrying on the tradition of the Wind God Festival said to have begun in 675 CE under Emperor Tenmu. Daytime rites are followed by an evening fireworks display known as the "Wind God Fireworks."
- **Reitaisai (typically April)**: A spring festival that carries on the tradition of the ancient Wind God Festival's spring observance, originally fixed on the 4th day of the 4th month.
- **Monthly goshuin and seasonal offerings**: Limited-edition goshuin with a different design are sometimes offered each month, giving visitors a reason to return through the seasons.

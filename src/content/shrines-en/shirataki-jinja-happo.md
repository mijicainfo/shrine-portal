## History

Shirataki Shrine stands at the foot of the Shirakami Mountains in Happo, Yamamoto District, Akita Prefecture. In the Akita Shrine Agency's shrine search it is read "Shirataki-jinja" and has the popular name 不動山, and its address is 10 Hachimoritate, Happo (the name is written with the character 瀑, not 滝). Its greatest feature is the waterfall "Shirataki," described as 17 meters high, behind the grounds. The Akita Prefecture community site "Akita Genki Mura" (posted April 2010) says it is rare in Japan for a shrine precinct to have such a powerful waterfall.

According to the Shrine Agency's history, more than 1,100 years ago, in Ninju 3 during the reign of Emperor Montoku, the priest Jikaku Daishi Ennin visited this place on his travels. Struck by how pure the area below the waterfall on Mt. Hachimori was and how cold and clear the mountain air felt, he stayed in seclusion here. Judging the cascade to be a sacred place without equal in the world, he carved an image of Fudo (Acala) and placed it on the rock north of the waterfall, praying for the safety of the land; this is regarded as the founding. Later, the Akita Jonosuke is said to have held the shrine in deep reverence and to have donated shrine lands. It was first called "Fudo-sha" because of the Shinto-Buddhist syncretism of the time, was renamed Shirataki Shrine in the Meiji era, and was ranked as a village shrine (gosha) in Meiji 5. Nippon Tabi Magazine gives Ninju 3 as 853.

According to the Shrine Agency, the enshrined deities are five: Homusubi no Kami, Haniyama-hime no Kami, Amaterasu Sumeo-kami, Oyamatsumi-hiruko no Kami, and Takehaya-susanoo no Mikoto. Within the grounds are the auxiliary shrines Hachiman Shrine, Sugawara Shrine and Karamatsu Shrine. The Japanese Wikipedia adds that in the Edo period the shrine was designated the 28th temple of the Akita Six-District Thirty-Three Kannon Pilgrimage, and that in Meiji 43 thirty shrines of the former Hachimori town were merged into it (for the merger it cites the Akita Jinja Meikan).

## Highlights

The main sight is the "Shirataki" waterfall falling behind the shrine hall. According to "Akita Genki Mura," the mist from the male and female falls creates a cool, refreshing atmosphere, and the place has even been introduced in the American magazine LIFE. The Edo-period travel writer Sugae Masumi is also said to have visited and looked down on the falls from atop the rocks. In the same article the shrine's priest recommends viewing the falls through the window of the shrine hall, saying it looks like a painting.

Akita Shirakami Tourism's page says the summer mist is refreshing and that in winter the waterfall often freezes into dramatic icicles. The pool in front of the falls is also where the mikoshi is carried in at the annual festival.

On the order of worship, Nippon Tabi Magazine quotes the chief priest as saying visitors should first pay respects to the enshrined deities in the shrine hall and then go to the waterfall, and that going to the waterfall alone, without greeting the deities, is discourteous. It adds that the shrine hall doors are kept unlocked at all times.

## Visiting Tips

No official site could be found, so set visiting hours and the number of parking spaces could not be confirmed. Akita Shirakami Tourism's page says it is open year-round and that free parking is available. The same page says resident priests are present only at special festivals. The contact number is 0185-77-2337 in the Shrine Agency's shrine search and 0185-77-3056 on the Tohoku Tourism Promotion Organization's page; the sources differ, so use both as a guide if you check before going.

Goshuin posts appear on a site where visitors post their own records, but no notice from the shrine itself was found, and the hours and fee are unconfirmed. If you want one, it is safest to call ahead.

It is about a 10–20 minute walk from Higashi-Hachimori Station on the JR Gono Line (sources differ). By car, turn right at the "Shirataki Jinja Iriguchi" sign about 30 minutes from Noshiro-Minami IC on the Akita Expressway along Route 101 toward Aomori (Tohoku Tourism Promotion Organization).

"Akita Genki Mura" (2010) says some people come to stand under the falls even in winter. Whether general visitors are accepted for this, and what the rules are, cannot be told from the sources checked, so ask the shrine beforehand.

## Annual Festivals

- **Annual festival "Mikoshi no Takiabi" (August 1)**: A rite the Akita Shrine Agency also lists as a special ritual. According to the Tohoku Tourism Promotion Organization, the mikoshi is carried around town and then into the waterfall pool, and hundreds of spectators gather around the falls to photograph this event, which is unusual even nationwide. According to the Akita Shirakami Tourism page and the Japanese Wikipedia, men in white robes carry the mikoshi around town from 7 a.m.; Akita Shirakami Tourism says it enters the pool around 1:30 p.m., while the Japanese Wikipedia says just after noon (the sources differ on the time). According to the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism's multilingual explanation database, the custom began when, in one very hot summer, the bearers cooled off in the waterfall behind the shrine while carrying the mikoshi; only men may take part, and white robes are required. Food and game stalls are cleared the day before, so that only the procession and the waterfall entry take place. The Akita Shirakami Tourism page says prayers are offered for a bountiful harvest, safe voyages, business prosperity and more. The Tohoku Tourism Promotion Organization's page also reports a shortage of bearers in recent years because of aging and population decline.

This festival is the only annual event whose date could be confirmed in this research. Times and details can change from year to year, so check with the town's tourist information or the shrine before going to watch.

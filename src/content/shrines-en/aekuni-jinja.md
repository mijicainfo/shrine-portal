## History

Aekuni Jinja stands in the Ichinomiya district of Iga City, Mie Prefecture, and is regarded as the foremost ancient shrine of former Iga Province. Shrine tradition places its founding in 658 CE, during the reign of Empress Saimei, though the surviving accounts of its origin are not backed by firm historical documentation. Its principal deity, Ōhiko-no-Mikoto, was the eldest son of the 8th Emperor, Kōgen, and according to the Nihon Shoki was dispatched as one of the four Shido Shogun generals to pacify the Hokuriku region. His descendants are said to have settled in the Abai district of Iga Province, which is the reason given for his enshrinement here.

The shrine appears in the Engishiki shrine registry, compiled in 927, listed as "Aekuni Jinja, Tai (Major)" in Abai District, Iga Province — a ranking held by no other shrine in the province. This distinction underlies its long-standing status as Iga's Ichinomiya. Under the Meiji-era shrine ranking system it was classified as a Kokuhei-chūsha (mid-ranked national shrine), and today it holds Beppyo status under the Association of Shinto Shrines.

During the Tenshō Iga War of 1581, the shrine's buildings were burned by forces loyal to Oda Nobunaga. It was later rebuilt through the efforts of mountain ascetics and under the patronage of Tōdō Takatora, lord of Iga Ueno Castle; in the Genna era, Takatora is said to have positioned the rebuilt shrine to guard the castle's vulnerable northeast (kimon) quarter.

## Enshrined Deities

The principal deity, Ōhiko-no-Mikoto, is an imperial figure associated with the pacification of the Hokuriku region, and — through the settlement of his descendants, the Abe clan, in the area — also carries something of the character of a local tutelary founder-deity. He is joined by Sukunahikona-no-Mikoto, known as a deity of medicine, sake-brewing, and wisdom, and by Kanayama-hime-no-Mikoto, a metalworking deity also enshrined at Nangu Taisha, the Ichinomiya of Mino Province in present-day Gifu.

## The Iga Ninja Connection

Aekuni Jinja's tie to Iga's ninja heritage is not merely modern tourism branding — it rests on a documented historical basis. The shrine is an officially recognized constituent property of the Agency for Cultural Affairs' Japan Heritage certification, under the story "Shinobi no Sato: Iga and Koga" (The Ninja's Hometown). In the closing decades of the medieval period, governance in Iga passed largely out of the hands of formal authorities like the provincial shugo and into those of local warrior-landholders (dogō); the shrine's festival administration is thought to have served as a mechanism through which these landholders — later organized into the confederation known as the Iga-shu — built cohesion among themselves. Ninja residences are said to have been scattered in the surrounding area, and the shrine, as the tutelary shrine of the surrounding villages, appears to have doubled as a meeting ground for the region.

The shrine's best-documented connection is the Kurando Festival (Kurando Matsuri), once privately administered by the Hattori clan — the lineage that would later produce the noted ninja retainer Hattori Hanzō. Participants in the rite were, by tradition, limited to clan members, who dressed in black for the occasion — a custom sometimes cited as one origin of the black ninja garb popularized in later tradition. The festival lapsed for a long period before being revived in 1995, roughly 450 years after it had ceased, and today it is held annually on November 23 with a lion dance and ninja-themed performances.

It is worth noting that the Ninja Museum of Igaryu — a separate facility in nearby Ueno Park — and the wider ninja-tourism infrastructure of Iga Ueno's city center developed independently of the shrine itself, as modern attractions built around this same regional heritage; they are best understood as neighbors to Aekuni Jinja's own history rather than part of it.

## Highlights

The shrine preserves a Shishi Kagura (lion dance) designated an Intangible Folk Cultural Property of Mie Prefecture, said to trace its origins to the Keichō era (1596–1615) and regarded as the prototype for lion dances performed across the wider Iga region. It is presented at the Maizome Festival on January 3, the Maiage Festival on April 17, and the shrine's principal annual festival on December 5. The grounds also hold several subsidiary shrines, including Ōishi-sha, Musubi-sha, Kosazuke-sha, and Ichikishima-hime-sha, each associated with its own particular blessing.

## Visiting Tips

The shrine office is open 8:30–16:00, and prayer service reception runs 9:00–15:00 (last reception 14:30). The wooded grounds have a calm, unhurried atmosphere, and the shrine is easily reached by a short bus ride from Ueno-shi Station on the Iga Railway. Free parking for about 20 cars is available on-site, making a visit by car convenient as well. Visitors with flexible schedules may want to time a trip around the lion dance festivals or the Kurando Festival.

## Annual Festivals

- **Kurando Festival** (November 23): Once privately administered by the Hattori clan, this festival lapsed for centuries before being revived in 1995, roughly 450 years after it ceased. It is now held with a lion dance and ninja-themed performances — a rite unique to Aekuni Jinja among Iga's shrines.
- **Shishi Kagura Maizome Festival** (January 3) and **Maiage Festival** (April 17): Performances of the prefecturally designated lion dance.
- **Annual Festival (Reisai)** (December 5): The shrine's most important yearly rite.

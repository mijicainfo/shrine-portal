## History

Kattamine Shrine (Kattamine-jinja) stands in the hot-spring town of Togatta Onsen in Zao, Miyagi Prefecture. It is the "satomiya" (village shrine) paired with the shrine of the same name on the summit of Mt. Katta (Kattadake) in the Zao range, the "okumiya" (inner shrine). This page focuses on the satomiya in Togatta Onsen (Nakamachi, Togatta Onsen, Zao) and also introduces the summit okumiya. According to the Zao Town geosite guide, there are three Kattamine shrines in the town (on the Zao summit, in Togatta and in Miya), and the Miya shrine (Hakucho Daimyojin) is a different shrine.

The Zao Town guide names the predecessor of this shrine as Dakenobo, a Shugendo temple whose practitioners trained on Mt. Zao. In the Edo period it was known as Kinpusen Zaoji and oversaw the Zao Daigongen shrine on the summit; during winter, from October 8 to April 8, when the summit was snowed in, it also served as the "Zao Daigongen Okari no Miya," housing the sacred object. Because Zaoji Dakenobo and the summit shrine had long been closely linked, the two were merged after the Meiji-era order separating Shinto and Buddhism: it became Mikumari Shrine (enshrined deity: Zao no Okami) in 1872 and Kattamine Shrine (enshrined deities: Ama-no-Mikumari-no-Kami and Kuni-no-Mikumari-no-Kami) in 1875. The guide says "Kattamine" was the name used here before Zao Daigongen was brought in and the mountain came to be called Zao. The founding date could not be determined from the sources found.

When popular "Oyama Mairi" (Zao pilgrimage) became fashionable in the late Edo period, Dakenobo handled the main approach and guided many pilgrims up to Zao Daigongen on the summit. The Zao Town guide says the pilgrimage that set out from Togatta Onsen for the summit of Mt. Katta was a boom that lasted until after the war and greatly influenced the growth of the hot-spring town. The summit shrine is said to have stood originally on the summit of Mt. Kumano and to have been moved later to the summit of Mt. Katta.

## Highlights

At the entrance to the grounds stands a torii bearing a "Zao Daigongen" plaque, and the Zao Town guide notes that spring water from a hot spring source flows beside the entrance. It is a fitting sight for a shrine in a hot-spring town, and it recalls the history of the satomiya as the starting point of the Zao pilgrimage.

Inside the worship hall is an ema painting, "Keimeiko-zu," depicting a Zao pilgrimage in the Meiji era. According to the guide, it was dedicated in 1905 by the Miyagi Keimeiko, a Sendai pilgrimage association, and is designated a cultural property of Zao Town. Because it is kept inside the worship hall, visitors who wish to see it are said to need to ask the shrine in advance.

Climbing the terrace cliff behind the shrine brings you to Yunokami Shrine and others on Mt. Gongen, with a view over the hot-spring town and the Zao range. According to the guide, a walking trail runs from Kattamine Shrine through Mt. Gongen and Mt. Iwasaki to Togatta Park (about 1.5 km, about 60 minutes one way), but there are some fairly steep sections, so footwear matters.

The summit okumiya stands on Mt. Katta (about 1,758 m), a lava dome formed by volcanic activity roughly 220,000 years ago. The guide says that beside it stands a monument to Date Munetaka, who prayed on the summit during the great Kan'ei eruption about 400 years ago for the mountain to calm. It is close to the viewing point for Okama (the crater lake of Mt. Goshiki), so it combines easily with Zao sightseeing.

## Visiting Tips

No official visiting hours or shrine-office hours could be confirmed for the satomiya (and no official site was found). It is safest to check in advance, for example via the manager's contact number listed in the Zao Town geosite guide (0224-34-2620), about amulets and whether viewing is possible. Visitors who want to see the Keimeiko-zu are said to need to ask in advance.

A visitor report says goshuin are given at the satomiya's shrine office, and one personal blog gives ¥300, but no official information could be confirmed, so please treat it as a rough guide. The Zao Kodo Association's site says its "Oyama Mairi" pilgrimage offers a "prayer" goshuin at the satomiya and a "completion" goshuin at the okumiya on the summit of Mt. Katta, ¥500 each (from a 2021 article).

The okumiya cannot be reached while the Zao Echo Line is closed for winter. Miyagi Prefecture's notices say the winter 2025 closure began at 5:00 PM on November 4 and the road reopened at 11:00 AM on April 24, 2026. It is usually closed from early November to late April, but the dates vary by year, so check the latest road-restriction information before you go.

## Annual Festivals

- **Annual festival (May 5)**: Shrine-introduction sites and the Japanese Wikipedia give May 5 for the satomiya's annual festival. No official announcement could be confirmed.
- **Seasonal relocation of the sacred object**: The Zao Town guide says the sacred object was housed at the satomiya in winter (October 8 to April 8) in the Edo period, and that the tradition of relocation between the satomiya and okumiya continues today. The Japanese Wikipedia says the object now moves up to the okumiya around late April, timed to the opening of the Zao Echo Line, and returns to the satomiya in autumn in an October "sacred-object descent ceremony," but this could not be confirmed in primary sources from the shrine or the town. Dates vary by year, so check beforehand.
- **Zao Oyama Mairi**: According to the Zao Town guide, the Zao Kodo trail was restored by local volunteers in 2014 and the "Zao Oyama Mairi" pilgrimage is held. The Zao Kodo Association's site had announced the 2026 edition as the 13th. Check the association's site for dates and how to join.
- **Oyu-kake Festival at Yunokami Shrine (Doyo no Ushi no Hi in July)**: A festival held by the Togatta Onsen Ryokan Association to coincide with the festival day of Yunokami Shrine on Mt. Gongen (per the Zao Town geosite guide).

Festival dates can change from year to year, so check the latest information with the shrine or Zao Town.

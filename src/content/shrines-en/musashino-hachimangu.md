## History

Musashino Hachimangu stands on Itsukaichi-kaido in Kichijoji-Higashicho, north of JR Kichijoji Station in Musashino, Tokyo. Its name is read "Musashino Hachimangu," and it is the beloved tutelary shrine of Kichijoji. According to the Tokyo Shrine Agency listing, the enshrined deities are Homutawake no Mikoto, Hime Okami and Otarashihime no Mikoto. Homutawake is commonly identified as Emperor Ojin and Otarashihime as Empress Jingu.

According to the Agency's history, in 789 (Enryaku 8), in the reign of Emperor Kanmu, Sakanoue no Tamuramaro is said to have enshrined a branch spirit of Usa Hachiman. Later, in the time of the fourth shogun Ietsuna, after a fire destroyed the Kichijoji temple near Suidobashi in Edo, nearby townspeople were ordered to resettle, and Kichijoji Village was founded in the early Kanbun era. The shrine has been revered as the villagers' tutelary shrine ever since. The name "Kichijoji" for the area is said to come from this resettlement. The founding date and circumstances come from the shrine's tradition and are not established historical fact.

The parishioner districts are given as Kichijoji-Higashicho, Honcho, Minamicho and Kitacho, plus Nakacho and Gotenyama (according to an interview article with the priest).

## Highlights

The grounds are not large, but they are wrapped in a calm grove that feels far from the town around it. The approach has a stone torii gate and a pair of guardian dogs, reported to date from around 1813 (Bunka 10) (according to an interview article with the priest).

Among its treasures is the "Warabitetō of Musashino Hachimangu," a Musashino City designated tangible cultural property (designated March 23, 1981, per the city's list of cultural properties). A warabitetō is an ancient sword whose hilt ends in a curl like a young bracken fern, and this one is described as having been unearthed in the grounds. In a 2015 city exhibition, the original was announced as being shown "this time only."

The grounds also hold a signpost dated 1785 (Tenmei 5), inscribed "Kanda Okamizu Inokashira Benzaiten," which points the way to Inokashira Benzaiten. Other features reported include subsidiary shrines (Izumo, Mishima, Itsukushima, Inari, Hōsō and Ōtori among them; guides count either six or seven), a portable-shrine house where you can see the mikoshi through glass, and early-blooming Kawazu cherry trees.

## Visiting Tips

No official website was found, and the Tokyo Shrine Agency listing gives no visiting hours. Goshuin and amulets are said to be handed out at the shrine office from 10:00 AM to 5:00 PM. The office is sometimes unattended, in which case you press the intercom beside the entrance.

The goshuin costs ¥300 and is normally hand-written in ink, or given as a pre-written sheet when the calligrapher is absent (according to an interview article with the priest and a visitor's account). Prayer services and Shichi-Go-San blessings are reportedly booked by phone (0422-22-5327), with Shichi-Go-San blessings available for about a month before and after November 15.

It is about a 10-minute walk from Kichijoji Station (per the Agency), though guides give 7 to 10 minutes. No dedicated parking is reported, so going by train is convenient. The New Year's visit is crowded from New Year's Eve through the first three days, and the priest recommends going during January or at least before Setsubun.

## Annual Festivals

- **Annual festival (September 15)**: The festival date given in the Tokyo Shrine Agency listing.
- **Kichijoji Autumn Festival (September)**: A town festival held to coincide with the annual festival, with the shrine's portable shrine and neighborhood mikoshi taking part (according to local information). Dates vary by year.
- **New Year's rite (January), Setsubun festival (February), Kinensai (May), Niiname-sai (November)**: Introduced as the main annual rites (according to an interview article with the priest; no dates given).
- **Tori no Ichi (Tori days in November)**: The Ōtori shrine in the grounds means a Tori no Ichi fair is held. The number of days varies from year to year, from the first to the third Tori day.
- **Musashino Kichijoji Seven Lucky Gods pilgrimage (New Year's Day to about January 7)**: The shrine is introduced as the Daikokuten stop among six temples and shrines in Musashino and Mitaka. Check the organizer's notice for details such as the fee.

Dates and details change from year to year, so please call the shrine for the latest information.

## History

Nagahama Hachimangu stands in Miyamae-cho, on the eastern side of the central district of Nagahama in Shiga. It is read "Nagahama Hachimangu," and the official site spells it with the older character form (長濱八幡宮). Known as the tutelary shrine of Nagahama, it enshrines, according to the official history, Tarashinakatsuhiko no Mikoto (Emperor Chuai; protection from misfortune and good fortune) in the east hall, Homuda Wake no Mikoto (Emperor Ojin; protection from misfortune, health and longevity) in the central hall, and Okinaga Tarashihime no Mikoto (Empress Jingu; guardian of safe childbirth) in the west hall.

The shrine's history says that in 1069 (Enkyu 1) Minamoto no Yoshiie, acting on an imperial vow of Emperor Gosanjo, brought a divided spirit from Iwashimizu Hachimangu in Kyoto and enshrined it here. The area came to be called the "Hachiman Estate," and the shrine was revered as the guardian deity of the eleven villages of the estate. It is said to have once held 3,000 koku of land and 73 temple-lodgings on the mountain, rivaling Iwashimizu itself, but repeated warfare destroyed most of its buildings. Then in Tensho 2 (1574), when Hashiba Hideyoshi became lord of Nagahama Castle, he is said to have lamented its decay and repaired and rebuilt the halls, working to revive the shrine. The official site says this event is also called the origin of the Nagahama Hikiyama Festival (some sources give 1573 for Hideyoshi's arrival). A tourism site also introduces the garden as a gift from Hideyoshi.

## Highlights

The main hall is built in the shinmei-zukuri style, which is rare for a Hachiman shrine, with a cypress-bark roof, chigi and katsuogi. The official site notes a tradition that it was taken over from the Mikeden (food-offering hall) of the Grand Shrine of Ise. A stone bridge and torii lead toward the worship hall, and pine trees line the approach, giving the grounds a calm feel.

Sessha and massha (auxiliary shrines) dot the precincts. The official site introduces Tsukubusuma Shrine, honoring Ichikishimahime no Kami (Benzaiten), the water goddess; Tenmangu, honoring Sugawara no Michizane; Kora Shrine, honoring Takeuchi no Sukune; and Kotohira-gu and Kawasogi Shrine, honoring Omononushi no Mikoto and others. Kora Shrine is billed as a shrine for longevity and is called the "Boke-fuji no Miya" (shrine to ward off senility), where a special amulet is given out.

Among the treasures, the official site introduces a wooden kyogen mask (inscribed with the year 1561, Muromachi period), Edo-period Noh costumes, and the decorative metal fittings of a portable shrine said to have been built in 1676. According to a tourism site, a Muromachi-period donation record is a Shiga Prefecture designated cultural property, and the shrine also holds a register signed jointly by Hideyoshi and his adopted son Hidekatsu (single source). The grounds also have a Noh stage, used for dedicatory performances during the year.

## Visiting Tips

Prayer services are accepted from 9:00 AM each morning with no reservation. The official site describes many kinds of prayers, including protection from misfortune and good fortune, household safety, safe childbirth, first shrine visits, Shichi-Go-San, Jusan-mairi and hoyoke. For people in an unlucky year there is the "Aratama no Yakunogare" special prayer, accepted only from January 18 to 29 (prayer fee ¥5,000).

The official site does not give visiting hours for the grounds, while a tourism site describes the shrine as open year-round with free entry. The goshuin fee and hours are also not stated on the official site, so they need to be confirmed. Visitor records mention hand-written goshuin, but the price is unverified. The shrine office (0749-62-0481) is the surest way to check.

The official site gives a walk of about 20 minutes from JR Nagahama Station, while a tourism site says about 15, so guides differ. It describes a route that passes Toyokuni Shrine and Kurokabe while going along Otemon-dori and Miyamachi-dori. There is a car park for about 50 regular cars on the grounds.

## Annual Festivals

The dates below follow the "Annual Festivals" calendar on the official site and can change from year to year.

- **Saitan-sai and New Year countdown (January 1)**: Chikuwa (fish-paste rolls) are thrown at midnight.
- **Setsubun Festival (around February 3)**: The Oni-yarai rite and bean-throwing.
- **Spring Grand Festival: portable-shrine procession (April 12) / Spring Grand Festival (Hikiyama festival) and the portable shrine's return (April 15)**: The spring festival that coincides with the Nagahama Hikiyama Festival. A tourism site says the Hikiyama Festival is held April 9–16 in a typical year, with the floats appearing and child kabuki performed from April 13 to 16. It is a nationally designated Important Intangible Folk Cultural Property and was inscribed on the UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage list in 2016.
- **Summer purification rite and chi-no-wa kuguri (June 30)**
- **Manto-sai and Hebi no Mai Shinji (August 14–16)**: The Hebi no Mai (serpent dance), offered on the evening of August 15 at the Hojo Pond, is listed on the official site as a Shiga Prefecture Intangible Folk Cultural Property. The site explains that no other offering is performed in water.
- **Annual festival (October 15)**: The Yawata-kosha grand festival, with a lottery and dedicatory performances among the accompanying events.
- **Niiname-sai (around November 23)** and **Joya-sai (December 31)**

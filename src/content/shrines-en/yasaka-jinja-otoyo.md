## History

According to shrine tradition, this Yasaka Jinja was founded in 912, when a warrior named Sugimoto Taro Yoshiie, who had traveled from Kyoto, enshrined three sacred images — Gion Gozu Tenno, Kifune Daimyojin, and the Buddhist figure Gyoki Bosatsu — at the base of an ancient cedar tree already standing on the site. Gozu Tenno was identified with Susanoo no Mikoto under the syncretic fusion of Shinto and Buddhism, and the shrine has been known as Yasaka Jinja ever since, serving as the guardian shrine of the Sugi district. It shares its name with the famous Yasaka Jinja of Gion in Kyoto, but the two are unrelated, independent shrines, each with its own distinct history.

The great cedar enshrined on the grounds, known as Sugi no Osugi, is said by local legend to have been planted by Susanoo no Mikoto himself in the ancient past, and is estimated to be more than 3,000 years old. It was designated a National Natural Monument in 1924, and its status was later upgraded to Special Natural Monument in 1952, making it one of the most revered giant trees in Japan.

## Highlights

The centerpiece of a visit is the great cedar itself, which splits into two massive trunks near its base. The southern trunk, "Minami-Osugi," measures about 20 meters in circumference and 60 meters in height, while the northern trunk, "Kita-Osugi," measures about 16.5 meters in circumference and 57 meters in height. Standing side by side, they are affectionately nicknamed the "Wedded Cedar" (Meoto-sugi) by locals, and together are considered the largest shrine-grown cedar in Japan, bound together with a thick sacred rope.

The site is equally known for its connection to Misora Hibari, one of postwar Japan's most beloved singers. On April 28, 1947, the then nine-year-old Hibari, on a regional performing tour, survived a serious accident when the bus she was riding in went off National Route 32 near this spot (then Osugi Village). While recovering, she is said to have prayed at the great cedar to become Japan's number one singer. Years later, at age 14, as her career began to take off, she returned to the area to greet those who had cared for her and renewed her vow at the tree. After her death, a memorial stone and song monument were erected beside the cedar in 1993, in a small memorial garden called Osugi no Sono. Visitors can still hear a recording of her singing at the monument, and the site remains a pilgrimage spot both for her fans and for those praying for success in the entertainment and arts world.

## Visiting Tips

The shrine grounds themselves are open to visitors at all times, but the fenced area for viewing the great cedar up close keeps set hours — 8:00 to 18:00 (8:30 to 17:00 from October through March) — and charges a facility maintenance fee of ¥200 for adults and ¥100 for elementary and middle school students. A flight of stone steps leads up from the roadside parking area on Route 32, so comfortable walking shoes are recommended. Allow about 30–45 minutes to see both the great cedar and the adjoining Misora Hibari memorial garden. Stopping first at Michi-no-Eki Osugi, the roadside station directly across the road, is a convenient way to pick up local tourist information before your visit.

## Annual Festivals

- **Great Cedar Shimenawa Rite (once a year)**: Local residents hand-braid a massive sacred rope — some 25–30 cm in diameter and 33–35 meters long — and ceremonially replace the old one binding the two trunks together, followed by a Shinto rite.
- **Osugi Summer Festival (summer)**: A community-run summer festival featuring live band performances and numerous food stalls, capped off with a fireworks display.
- **New Year's Visit (January 1–3)**: Local residents and visitors drawn to the great cedar's reputation as a power spot come to make the first shrine visit of the new year.

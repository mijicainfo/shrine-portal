## History

Tomioka Hachimangu's founding is traditionally dated to 1627, when a Kyoto monk named Choso is said to have received a divine oracle instructing him to enshrine Hachiman on Eitaijima, a stretch of land then being reclaimed from Edo Bay. He brought a divided spirit of the deity from the Tomioka Hachimangu shrine in Sagami Province (present-day Kanagawa) to establish the new shrine. At the time, the surrounding area was little more than a sandbar undergoing land reclamation, but as the shrine's buildings went up, a bustling approach town grew alongside it — laying the foundation for what would become the Fukagawa neighborhood.

Throughout the Edo period, Tomioka Hachimangu was affectionately known as "Fukagawa's Hachiman-sama" and grew into what was called the largest Hachiman shrine in Edo, drawing devotion from ordinary townspeople. In 1642, a ceremony was reportedly held on the grounds to celebrate the coming-of-age of Tokugawa Iemitsu's eldest son (the future fourth shogun, Ietsuna), reflecting the shrine's standing with the shogunal family as well.

In 1684, the Edo shogunate — which had repeatedly banned sumo exhibitions after years of trouble surrounding them — granted an exception allowing official kanjin-zumo tournaments to be held twice yearly, in spring and autumn, on the grounds of Tomioka Hachimangu. This marked the beginning of organized sumo in Edo, and the shrine is remembered today as the birthplace of modern professional sumo. Tournaments continued to be held here for roughly a century, during which time the regular tournament system and the ranking hierarchy that still define sumo took shape. Today the shrine is counted among the Tokyo Ten Shrines, and continues to be affectionately known in the Fukagawa neighborhood by its old nickname, "Fukagawa's Hachiman-sama."

## Highlights

The centerpiece of a visit to Tomioka Hachimangu is its collection of sumo-related stone monuments. Most striking is the Yokozuna Rikishi Monument, erected in 1900 at the initiative of the 12th yokozuna, Jinmaku Kyugoro — an imposing stone stele roughly 3.5 meters tall, 3 meters wide, and weighing some 20 tons. Carved into its surface are the ring names of every yokozuna in sumo history, from the very first, Akashi Shiganosuke, down to the present day. Whenever a new yokozuna is promoted, the Japan Sumo Association holds a formal naming ceremony before the monument, followed by the new champion's ring-entering ceremony — a tradition that continues to this day. A companion Ozeki Rikishi Monument stands nearby, giving the shrine grounds something of the air of a sumo pilgrimage site.

The grounds are also home to a bronze statue of Ino Tadataka, the surveyor renowned for creating Japan's first accurate maps of the entire country. Tadataka spent his later years living in Fukagawa, and it's said that before setting out on each of his nationwide surveying expeditions — which he began after the age of 50 — he would always visit Tomioka Hachimangu to pray for safe travels. The statue, erected in 2001 in his honor, depicts him holding a surveyor's staff fitted with a compass, and stands just inside the shrine's torii gate.

Another highlight is the antique flea market held on the grounds several times a month, drawing as many as 100 vendor stalls and crowds of treasure hunters. Paired with the neighboring Fukagawa Fudodo temple, the area offers a distinctly old-Tokyo, downtown atmosphere well suited to an afternoon of strolling.

## Visiting Tips

The shrine grounds can be visited freely at any time, but the office for goshuin and amulets closes at 17:00. Access is excellent — just 3 minutes on foot from Exit 1 of Monzen-nakacho Station — and one of the shrine's distinguishing features is how often visitors pair it with a stop at the neighboring Fukagawa Fudodo temple.

While touring the grounds, be sure not to miss the Yokozuna and Ozeki Rikishi Monuments and the Ino Tadataka statue in addition to the main worship hall. On flea market days (held on Sundays around the 1st, 15th, and 28th of most months — check the official site for exact dates) the grounds get lively with visitors, so a weekday visit is best if you're after a quieter experience. During the Fukagawa Matsuri in August, both the shrine grounds and the surrounding streets become extremely crowded and subject to traffic restrictions, so it's worth checking the official website for the latest access information beforehand.

## Annual Festivals

- **Fukagawa Hachiman Festival (Fukagawa Matsuri; mid-August, main festival every three years)**: Tomioka Hachimangu's grand annual festival, ranked alongside Hie Jinja's Sanno Matsuri and Kanda Myojin's Kanda Matsuri as one of Edo's three great festivals. It's also known as the "Water-Splashing Festival" because onlookers along the parade route douse the mikoshi bearers with purifying water as they pass. Once every three years, the festival's main event sees more than 50 large mikoshi from the parish neighborhoods paraded together through Fukagawa in a spectacular joint procession — the next main festival is in 2026.
- **New Yokozuna Ring-Entering Ceremony**: Whenever a wrestler is promoted to yokozuna, a naming ceremony at the Yokozuna Rikishi Monument is followed by a ring-entering ceremony performed by the new champion on the shrine grounds.
- **Antique Flea Market (held several times a month)**: An open-air antique market on the shrine grounds that draws treasure hunters from across the city — a beloved fixture of old-Tokyo Fukagawa life.
- **New Year's Visit (January)**: As "Fukagawa's Hachiman-sama," the shrine draws its largest crowds of the year from across Koto Ward and beyond during the New Year holiday.

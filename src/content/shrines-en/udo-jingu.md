## History

Udo Jingu is unlike almost any other shrine in Japan: its vermillion main hall is built directly inside a vast sea cave carved into the cliffs of the Nichinan coast. Shrine tradition dates its founding to the reign of the legendary Emperor Sujin, though the exact year is unknown. The enshrined deity, Hikonagisatakeugayafukiaezu no Mikoto, is the child of Hikohohodemi (Yamasachihiko) and Toyotama-hime, the daughter of the sea god, and figures directly in the "Umisachi-Yamasachi" (Luck of the Sea, Luck of the Mountains) myth cycle at the heart of Japan's founding legends.

According to the myth, Toyotama-hime built a birthing hut inside this very cave, thatching its roof with cormorant feathers so she could give birth in secrecy. Before the roof could be finished, the child was born — hence the name Ugayafukiaezu, meaning "not yet thatched." This child would grow up to become the father of Emperor Jimmu, Japan's first emperor, making Udo Jingu a site tied directly to the origins of the imperial line. Until the Meiji-era separation of Buddhism and Shinto, the site was also a Shingon Buddhist temple, and traces of that syncretic history remain woven into its story.

## Highlights

The undisputed highlight of Udo Jingu is its main hall, tucked beneath the low ceiling of a dramatic sea cave with waves crashing against the rocks just beyond its vermillion fence. Walking down the approach and watching the ocean view open up through a tunnel of red torii gates, with the roar of the surf growing louder, is unlike the approach to any ordinary shrine. In front of the main hall sits Kame-Iwa, a turtle-shaped rock formation; visitors throw small clay balls called "undama" (fortune balls) — men with their left hand, women with their right — aiming for a rope-marked circle on the turtle's back, said to grant good fortune to those who succeed.

The grounds also hold "Ochichi-iwa," a rock said to be where Toyotama-hime left behind two breast-shaped stones after giving birth, drawing visitors who pray for safe childbirth, healthy children, and good relationships. The Chidori Bridge along the approach and the long descent through consecutive red torii gates toward the cave are also well worth taking in slowly.

## Visiting Tips

The round trip from the parking area to the main hall and back takes about 30–45 minutes over a gently sloping path and some stone steps. The cave floor near the main hall can be damp and slippery, so comfortable, non-slip shoes are recommended. Undama fortune balls can be purchased at the stand just before the cave, typically five balls for about ¥100. Goshuin can be received at the shrine office near the approach entrance or at the counter inside the cave. On clear days, the contrast between the deep blue Hyuga Sea and the vermillion shrine buildings is especially striking, and low tide makes the area around Kame-Iwa easier to approach.

## Annual Festivals

- **New Year's Festival (January 1)**: The first festival of the year, praying for safety and prosperity in the year ahead.
- **Regular Grand Festival (February 1)**: One of the most solemn and important festivals held at Udo Jingu each year.
- **Spring Grand Festival & Shanshan-uma Procession (late March)**: Reenacts the Edo-period custom in which newlywed couples rode a bell-adorned horse to the shrine, accompanied by a folk song competition.
- **Niiname-sai Thanksgiving Festival (November 23)**: A major autumn rite offering thanks for the year's harvest.

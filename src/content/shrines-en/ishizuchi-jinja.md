## History

Ishizuchi Jinja is an ancient shrine venerating Mount Ishizuchi (1,982 meters), the highest peak in western Japan and one of Japan's Seven Sacred Mountains, as its sacred object of worship. According to shrine tradition, the mountain was first opened to religious practice in 650 (Hakuchi 1) by En no Gyoja, the semi-legendary founder of Shugendo, the path of Japanese mountain asceticism. From that point on, Mount Ishizuchi became one of the great centers of mountain worship and Shugendo practice, drawing devoted pilgrims from across Shikoku and western Japan.

The enshrined deity is Ishizuchi-hiko no Mikoto, also known as Ishitsuchi-hiko no Mikoto or Ishizuchi Okami, said to be a child of the primordial creator deities Izanagi no Mikoto and Izanami no Mikoto. In earlier centuries, the mountain itself was worshipped directly as a god, and under the syncretic fusion of Shinto and Buddhism the site became a major Buddhist mountain-training center as well, drawing ascetics and pilgrims from across the region. With the Meiji-era edict separating Shinto and Buddhism, the shrine was reorganized into its present Shinto form as Ishizuchi Jinja — though the older ascetic tradition survives vividly today in the mountain's chain-climbing routes and in its summer Oyama-biraki festival.

## The Four Shrines of Ishizuchi Jinja

The single most distinctive feature of Ishizuchi Jinja is that it isn't one building but four linked sites, spaced out along the pilgrimage route from the mountain's foot to its summit. Understanding this structure up front makes a visit far less confusing.

- **Kuchinomiya Honsha** (the main shrine): Located at the mountain's base, a short walk from Ishizuchisan Station, this is the site nearly all visitors actually reach. Its grounds include the main hall, a shrine gate, an ancestral hall, and a spring of sacred water. It's open year-round and, for a typical visit or sightseeing trip, this is the only site you need to see.
- **Chugu Joju-sha** (the mid-mountain shrine): Set at the 7th station partway up the mountain, about a 20-minute walk from the top of the Ishizuchi Ropeway. It takes its name — "the shrine of fulfillment" — from the legend of En no Gyoja finally succeeding in opening the mountain after being encouraged by an old man sharpening an axe. It serves as a key staging point for climbers heading toward the summit, and most visitors reach it by ropeway.
- **Tsuchigoya Yohaiden** (the distant-worship hall): Built in 1971 at Tsuchigoya, the end of the Ishizuchi Skyline (also known as the UFO Line) mountain road. It serves climbers approaching the summit from the ridge route opposite the Joju-sha side.
- **Chojo-sha** (the summit shrine): The innermost and highest of the four, standing atop Mount Ishizuchi's Misen peak, within sight of the jagged Tengudake spire just beyond — the literal high point of the pilgrimage. Reaching it requires proper mountaineering preparation and stamina.

In short, "Ishizuchi Jinja" doesn't mean a single building — it's the collective name for four places of worship arranged around the sacred mountain itself, and keeping that structure straight is the key to understanding the shrine.

## The Chain-Climbing Tradition

Mount Ishizuchi is one of the few sacred mountains in Japan where the old Shugendo chain-climbing routes, known as kusari-ba, remain in active use today. Four iron-chain ascents lace the upper mountain: the "trial chain" near Maeshagamori (about 48 meters), the first chain (about 33 meters), the second chain (about 65 meters), and the third chain (about 68 meters), each bolted into a near-vertical rock face.

These chains preserve a genuine ascetic-training tradition, but they are also open to ordinary hikers today, and each one has a bypass path (maki-michi) for those who'd rather skip the climb. That said, don't underestimate them: the actual chain routes are a serious, near-vertical scramble on sparse footholds, relying entirely on the chain itself for support, and conditions become genuinely hazardous when the rock is wet or winds pick up. Proper hiking shoes, gloves, and both hands free are strongly recommended, and first-timers or anyone uncertain of their fitness and experience should take the bypass paths, or climb with an experienced partner or guide, rather than push through the chains. In winter (roughly December through April) snow and ice make the summit approach — chains or otherwise — effectively impassable without full winter mountaineering gear.

## Oyama-biraki, the Mountain-Opening Festival

Ishizuchi Jinja's largest annual event is Oyama-biraki, held over ten days starting July 1. In the pre-dawn hours of June 30, a departure rite is held at the Kuchinomiya Honsha, after which three sacred images — representing benevolence (jin), wisdom (chi), and courage (yu) — are carried up the mountain on believers' backs, with the ascent toward the summit Chojo-sha beginning at 7:00 AM on July 1. Believers and climbers converge on Mount Ishizuchi from across Japan for what is by far the biggest event in the mountain's ritual calendar, crossing the chain-climbing sections along the way. One point worth noting when planning a visit: by long-standing tradition, July 1 itself is closed to women climbers.

## Goshuin

A goshuin can be received at all four sites — the Kuchinomiya Honsha, the Joju-sha, the Tsuchigoya Yohaiden, and the Chojo-sha — each with its own distinct design. Because coordinating hand-written seals across four separate, often remote locations isn't practical, every site issues only a pre-printed (kakioki) goshuin rather than writing directly into a visitor's book. At the Kuchinomiya Honsha the fee is ¥500, with reception hours running roughly 8:30 AM to 5:00 PM. Staffing at the other three sites is seasonal — the Joju-sha is staffed year-round, the Tsuchigoya Yohaiden from April 1 through late November, and the summit Chojo-sha only from May 1 through November 3 — so anyone planning to collect goshuin from multiple sites should check the season in advance.

## Access & Visiting Information

The Kuchinomiya Honsha is about a 10-minute walk from Ishizuchisan Station on the JR Yosan Line, or about 10 minutes by bus from Iyo-Saijo Station. By car, it's roughly 15 minutes from either the Iyo-Saijo or Iyo-Komatsu interchange on the Matsuyama Expressway. Free parking for about 350 vehicles is available on the grounds, and there is no admission fee to visit. The grounds themselves are open all day, though the shrine office handling goshuin and amulets keeps hours of roughly 8:30 AM to 5:00 PM.

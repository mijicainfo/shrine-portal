## History

Kishu Toshogu (Kishu-toshogu) stands on a small hill in Wakaura-nishi, Wakayama City, overlooking the scenic Wakaura coast. The official site says it has long been known affectionately as "Gongen-san."

According to the history on the official site, in 1621 (Genna 7) Tokugawa Yorinobu, the first lord of the Kishu domain and the tenth son of Tokugawa Ieyasu, founded the shrine at Wakaura to enshrine his father, pray for peace in the land and the well-being of the people, and serve as the chief guardian shrine of the Nankaido. The official site names Tokugawa Ieyasu and Tokugawa Yorinobu as the enshrined deities. It explains that Ieyasu's divine title is "Toshogu Daigongen" and that his honji-butsu (original Buddhist form) is said to be Yakushi Nyorai (Bhaisajyaguru), so he is also revered as a deity who heals illness.

The official site says the shrine, which retains traces of the Momoyama style, was designated a national Important Cultural Property as an early Edo-period building, that it has a history of about four hundred years, and that it has been celebrated in the saying "Wakanoura has famous sights, and Gongen is the first." The official site describes the shrine buildings, decorated with carvings by Hidari Jingoro and paintings of the Kano and Tosa schools, as worthy of the name "Nikko of the Kansai."

## Highlights

Going along the approach, you come to a straight flight of stone steps leading up to the grounds. According to the official site, this slope is called "Samurai-zaka," and there are 108 steps in all. Because the stones are large, visitors who find the steep climb hard are directed to the gentler staircase on the left of the slope. Once you reach the top and turn your back on the vermilion Ro-mon gate, the townscape of the scenic Wakaura opens out below. The official site says the vermilion-lacquered, richly colored Ro-mon is "said to be the finest in the Kansai."

The shrine buildings are richly colored, with carvings by Hidari Jingoro and wall paintings of the Kano and Tosa schools. According to the official site, fundamental repairs are carried out every 50 years in a regular cycle, and colored-finish repairs at 10- and 20-year intervals, keeping the buildings as they were when founded. The official site describes carvings of a hawk and pheasant (recalling Ieyasu's love of falconry), a dragon and tiger on the front pillars (the tiger is a symbol because Ieyasu was born in the year, day and hour of the tiger), a red carp and black carp on the east side (tied to the Toryumon legend and said to lead to success in life), and a crane and tortoise on the west (said to bring long life), with openwork carvings of a phoenix and four heavenly maidens cut from zelkova on the reverse of the front pillars.

The Agency for Cultural Affairs' database (Bunka Isan Online) lists the Toshogu main hall, Ishi-no-ma and worship hall, the Kara-mon gate, the east and west Mizugaki fences, the Ro-mon gate and the east and west corridors (Wakayama City, Wakaura) as Important Cultural Properties, all with the date 1621. The designation dates given are May 24, 1916 for the main hall, Ishi-no-ma and worship hall, the Kara-mon and the Mizugaki, and May 29, 1965 for the Ro-mon and corridors.

The grounds also hold the Inari Daimyojin shrine, the mikoshi house and the Benzaiten shrine (per the official site's map of the grounds). There is also a "fu no ki" tree associated with the eighth shogun Yoshimune; because its old leaves stay until new ones appear, it is popular with exam candidates as a "tree that does not fall," says the official site.

## Visiting Tips

Gate and visiting hours are 9:00 AM–4:30 PM, and admission is ¥300 for adults and ¥100 for elementary to high school students (paid at the shrine office on the hilltop). Groups of 30 or more get a 10% discount, and a guided explanation by a priest or miko costs ¥500 per person (admission included). When a volunteer guide is on duty, you can be shown around the shrine buildings free of charge (admission still applies). The official site says admission is free for residents of the Wakaura district, for Wakayama City residents with an "Omairi-ken" ticket, for holders of a disability certificate and others. Commercial photography and video within the grounds are prohibited, and permission must be obtained in advance if you bring an outside photographer (casual personal snapshots are not strictly restricted, the shrine says).

Prayer services include protection from misfortune, safe childbirth, first shrine visits for babies, Shichi-Go-San, traffic safety, academic success, recovery from illness and victory, with a prayer fee of ¥8,000 or more (¥10,000 or more for the first shrine visit and for kamidana purification). Reserve by phone; on the day, register at the Toshogu Kaikan right next to the grounds' parking lot. A "remote prayer" service is also available for those who cannot visit.

The official site says goshuin, ofuda, omamori, ema and other items are available, but it gives no offering amount or hours for the goshuin. Checking with the shrine office (073-444-0808) beforehand is reassuring.

The bus is convenient: about 20 minutes by Wakayama Bus bound for "Shin-Wakaura" from Nankai Wakayama-shi or JR Wakayama Station, then about a 1-minute walk from "Gongen-mae" (Wakayama City's guide). There are 70 parking spaces, with large buses by reservation.

## Annual Festivals

- **Waka Matsuri (annual festival)**: According to Wakayama City, the highlights are the "mikoshi-oroshi," in which the mikoshi runs down the shrine's 108 stone steps, and a procession with drums, Saika odori dance and naginata performances. The official Wakayama tourism site describes a large procession of about 1,000 people. In 2026 it was held on Sunday, May 17, with the mikoshi descent at 11:00 and the procession from 12:00. The next date is not given in the sources I could confirm, so check Wakayama City's guide or the Toshogu Kaikan (073-444-0808) before you go.

Festival dates change from year to year, so check the "News" page on the official site for the latest.

## History

Sakatsura Isosaki Jinja traces its founding to December 29, 856, according to shrine tradition. A salt maker working on the coast of what is now Oarai, in Kashima District of ancient Hitachi Province, is said to have become possessed by a divine spirit that proclaimed: "I am Onamuchi and Sukunahikona. Having completed the creation of this land, I departed for the eternal realm, but I have returned to save the people." This miraculous event is said to have led to the near-simultaneous founding of two shrines on opposite banks of the Naka River: Oarai Isosaki Jinja on the north bank, enshrining Onamuchi, and Sakatsura Isosaki Jinja on the south bank, in what is now Isozaki, Hitachinaka, enshrining Sukunahikona. The following year, in 857, the Imperial court granted both shrines official state-shrine status and the divine title "Sakatsura Isosaki Yakushi Bosatsu Myojin." Sakatsura Isosaki Jinja was later ranked among the Myojin Taisha of the Engi system, and in 1885 was designated a Kokuhei Chusha (national shrine of middle rank).

Like many shrines, it fell into disrepair amid the conflicts of the medieval period, but was restored during the Edo period under the patronage of the Mito domain. Tokugawa Mitsukuni, the domain's second lord, is credited with having camellias planted along the approach as part of the shrine's restoration — trees that survive today as a grove of specimens over 300 years old.

The shrine's principal deity is Sukunahikona no Mikoto, with Onamuchi no Mikoto enshrined alongside as a secondary deity. Sukunahikona is honored as a god of medicine, sake brewing, and hot springs, worshipped for centuries for blessings of recovery from illness and long, healthy life. As the shrine facing Oarai Isosaki Jinja across the mouth of the Naka River, sharing a common founding legend, the two are often described as "brother shrines," and many visitors make a point of paying respects at both.

## Highlights

One of Sakatsura Isosaki Jinja's most distinctive features is its roughly 300-meter approach, lined on both sides with camellia and tabu trees said to have been planted on the orders of Tokugawa Mitsukuni, the second lord of the Mito domain. Trees over 300 years old form a tunnel-like canopy overhead, and this broadleaf grove — including camellia, tabu, and chinquapin trees — was designated an Ibaraki Prefecture Natural Monument in 2005. The camellias bloom from winter into early spring, drawing visitors and photographers to walk beneath the flowering canopy.

The stone "Lucky Turtle" statue in front of the worship hall is another highlight not to be missed. Tradition holds that it was donated by a visitor who went on to win a large lottery prize after praying here, and touching the turtle is now widely believed to bring financial luck — a reputation that has made the shrine a frequent subject of television and magazine features as one of Japan's foremost "money luck" power spots. The shrine hall also features carvings of a squirrel and grapes attributed to Hidari Jingoro, the master sculptor famed for the "Sleeping Cat" at Nikko Toshogu, showcasing the refined woodcarving of the Edo period. The grounds include two torii gates facing the Pacific and several sites tied to the Mito domain, including a stone said to have been used as a seat by the ninth lord, Tokugawa Nariaki, during a horse-racing ritual.

## Visiting Tips

The grounds have no closing time and can be visited at any hour, but the shrine office for amulets and goshuin is open 8:00–16:00. Prayer requests (gokito) are accepted 9:00–15:00, and same-day requests are welcome. Free parking for about 60 vehicles is available on the east side of the grounds, though it can fill up during the New Year period, Setsubun, Shichi-Go-San, and the hydrangea and camellia blooming seasons, so allowing extra time is a good idea. Visitors combining a trip with neighboring Oarai Isosaki Jinja should allow about 15–20 minutes to drive between the two, across the Naka River.

## Annual Festivals

- **Reitaisai Grand Festival (typically August 25)**: The shrine's most important festival, held with an envoy from the Association of Shinto Shrines; some years include additional celebrations such as Noh performances.
- **Setsubun Festival (around February 3)**: A ritual to drive away misfortune and pray for good health in the year ahead.
- **Hiraiso Sansha Festival (once every three years, in August)**: A joint palanquin procession held together with the other shrines of the Hiraiso district, one of the area's largest community festivals.

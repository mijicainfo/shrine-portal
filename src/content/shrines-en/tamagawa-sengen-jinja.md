## History

Tamagawa Sengen Jinja stands on a small hill near Tamagawa Station in Denenchofu 1-chome, Ota, Tokyo. The enshrined deity is Konohanasakuya-hime no Mikoto, described on the official site as the princess daughter of Oyamatsumi, the god who protects mountains. The official site gives no furigana for the shrine's name, so this page follows Japanese Wikipedia's reading, "Tamagawa Sengen Jinja" (the official English page spells it "Tamagawa Sengen Shrine").

According to the official history, the shrine is said to have been founded eight hundred years ago. In the Bunji era of the Kamakura period (1185–90), when Minamoto no Yoritomo set out for battle, his wife Hojo Masako followed him as far as this area but hurt her foot in her straw sandals and had to rest and treat the injury on the bank of the Tama River. During her stay she climbed Kamenokoyama and saw Mt. Fuji clearly. She prayed toward the Sengen shrine at Fujiyoshida, her personal guardian deity, for her husband's success in battle, and set up the Seikanzenon statue she carried on this hill. The villagers revered the statue as "Fuji Sengen Daibosatsu," and this is said to be the origin of the shrine. The Ota City page "Sengen Shrine and Sengen Shrine Kofun" also says the shrine is traditionally said to have begun when Masako enshrined her personal Kannon statue here.

The official site adds that in May of the first year of Jōō (1652), during retaining-wall work on the front slope, a bronze standing statue of Seikanzenon was dug up near the ninth station of the hill. When the mud was washed off in the Tama River it turned out to be missing a leg, so a leg was cast and the statue was enshrined, with a Shinto rite held on June 1; since then the festival has been held in June. It also explains that Shimonumabe (下沼部) village once had three shrines (Sengen, Akagi and Kumano), and that after a government order of 1907 (Meiji 40) for "one shrine per village," the villagers discussed the matter and Sengen Shrine became the new village's guardian shrine. All of this is the shrine's own account.

## Highlights

The current hall was completed in 1973 (Showa 48), and the official guide to the grounds calls it an "elegant Sengen-zukuri" building and the only one in Tokyo (it is generally written as Sengen-zukuri 浅間造). The official site says the central one of the shrine's three crests is a cherry blossom, after the enshrined deity.

The shrine also has a link to an ancient tomb. According to the Ota City page, human and animal haniwa dating from the late 5th to early 6th century were unearthed when the hall was rebuilt, and the tomb, estimated to be a keyhole-shaped mound 60 m long, is called the "Sengen Jinja Kofun." Japanese Wikipedia says the hall stands on its rear circular mound. In addition, the "Omamori" page of the official site says the shrine was used as the command post for the "Taba Operation" in the 2016 film シン・ゴジラ (Shin Godzilla), and that a commemorative Godzilla ema-and-amulet set and other items are distributed at the office (as of the November 2026 check).

The grounds hold a hilltop viewing deck, the monument to 食行身禄 (Jikigyo Miroku, regarded as the reviver of the Fuji-ko confraternities) inscribed by Katsu Kaishu, the stone wheel 大祓詞石車 (turning it once around counts as reciting the great purification prayer), the stone 子産石 said to bring children when stroked, the crystal ball 開運水晶玉 (turn its base to point your zodiac sign toward Mt. Fuji and pray for good fortune), and the "takekurabe" (height-comparison) monument where families take photos. The auxiliary shrines enshrine 三峯神社, 小御嶽神社 and 阿夫利神社 (mountain worship) along with an Inari shrine.

## Visiting Tips

Opening hours for the grounds are not stated on the official site. The office is open 10:00 AM to 4:00 PM; to get amulets or goshuin, climb the steps from the torii gate and go to the office to the right of the main hall. Goshuin are distributed 10:00 AM–4:00 PM, and on some days only pre-written goshuin are available. The fee is not stated on the official site, so please check at the office.

Prayer services are accepted 10:00 AM–3:30 PM (every 30 minutes) and are by phone reservation made by the day before in principle. In busy periods such as New Year's and Shichi-Go-San (designated periods in October–November) no reservation is needed, but the prayers are held jointly. The official site lists prayer fees of ¥10,000 for a first-shrine-visit prayer and ¥7,000 for other prayers such as safe childbirth, Shichi-Go-San and protection from misfortune. The official site explains that because the deity gave birth amid flames, she is revered as a god of safe childbirth, children and harmonious households.

The official site says it is about a 2-minute walk from Tamagawa Station (the Ota City page says about 5 minutes, so the two differ). There are about 20 parking spaces; by car, head north at the Maruko Bridge intersection, and the lot is up the slope.

## Annual Festivals

The official site's annual events list includes the following. Dates can change from year to year, so check the official site for the latest.

- **New Year's Day rite (元旦祭) (January 1)**
- **Setsubun Tsuina ceremony (February 3), Hatsuuma-sai (a Day of the Horse in February), Kigensetsu rite (February 11)**
- **Annual festival (June 1)**: The history says the festival has been held in June since the first-year-of-Jōō rite.
- **Yoimiya-sai and Reitaisai (first Saturday and Sunday of June)**: The official site says photos of the 2026 (Reiwa 8) festival were posted on June 17.
- **Oharae-sai, the great purification (June 30 and December 31)**
- **Chu-sai (August 15)**
- **Shichi-Go-San festival (November 15)**
- **Niiname-sai (November 23, Labor Thanksgiving Day)**: As of the official site in November 2026.
- **Joya-sai, New Year's Eve rite (December 31)**

## History

Mana-i Jinja (also written Manai Jinja) is the okunomiya, or inner shrine, of Moto-Ise Kono Jinja, standing at the foot of the Amanohashidate sandbar in Miyazu, Kyoto Prefecture. Kono Jinja itself is known as a "Moto-Ise" — a place where Amaterasu-Omikami and Toyouke-Omikami are said to have resided before their eventual, permanent enshrinement at Ise Jingu — and Mana-i-hara, the plain where Mana-i Jinja stands today, is regarded as the very source of that tradition.

Shrine tradition holds that Toyouke-Omikami was enshrined here in ancient times, and that Amaterasu-Omikami was later welcomed to this same site as well. Another strand of tradition tells of Amenomurakumo-no-Mikoto, an ancestor of the Amabe clan that has served as Kono Jinja's hereditary priestly family, descending to this spot carrying the sacred water of the heavenly Mana-i well in a golden vessel. In later reigns, Amaterasu-Omikami is said to have journeyed on to Ise, and Toyouke-Omikami to have been invited to Ise's Outer Shrine as well — the layered tradition behind the name "Moto-Ise." Even after the deities' departure for Ise, a shrine continued to be maintained at Mana-i-hara, and it is preserved today as the okunomiya of Kono Jinja.

## Highlights

The single most striking feature of Mana-i Jinja is the iwakura — an ancient sacred rock formation preserved in its original state directly behind the main hall. Predating the construction of shrine buildings altogether, it represents one of Japan's oldest known forms of nature worship, in which people venerated bare rock outcrops as the dwelling place of the gods. The iwakura is divided into two seats: the primary seat, enshrining Toyouke-Omikami, and the western seat, enshrining Amaterasu-Omikami together with Izanagi-no-Okami and Izanami-no-Okami. Stone tools and scrapers from the Jomon period, along with magatama beads and fragments of miniature ritual pottery from the Yayoi period, have been excavated in the surrounding area — physical evidence that this site has been used for ritual purposes since prehistoric times.

Within the grounds, a sacred spring called Ame no Mana-i no Mizu ("the heavenly Mana-i water") flows continuously, said to be the very water that Amenomurakumo-no-Mikoto carried down from the heavens. It is considered one of the sources behind the many "Mana-i" and "Ama-no-Mana-i" place names and shrine names found across Japan. The iwakura itself, however, is treated as forbidden ground: visitors can approach only as far as the sacred fence (tamagaki) that now marks its boundary, beyond which entry and photography are both prohibited.

## Visiting Tips

The grounds are open 8:00–17:00 (subject to change). Mana-i Jinja has no shrine office of its own for most of the month, so goshuin are normally obtained at Kono Jinja's shrine office at the base of the hill (roughly 9:00–16:30). Mana-i Jinja's own office is staffed only a few designated days each month, with the dates announced on Kono Jinja's official website.

Beyond the second torii, the stone steps up to the main hall and the iwakura precinct behind it are off-limits — both entry and photography are prohibited there, a rule that has been enforced more strictly in recent years as the shrine's fame as a "power spot" has drawn larger crowds. The approach and the area at the base of the stairs remain free to photograph, but be sure to follow any signage or staff instructions once inside the restricted zone. There is no dedicated parking at Mana-i Jinja, so visitors arriving by car should park at Kono Jinja (the first 30 minutes are free, then ¥800 for the day) and walk up the roughly 10-minute approach path.

## Annual Festivals

- **Okunomiya Mana-i Jinja Reisai (October 15)**: Mana-i Jinja's own annual festival, timed to echo the traditional date of the full moon of the ninth lunar month, honoring Toyouke-Omikami in her aspects as a deity of water and of the moon.
- **Kono Jinja Reisai (April 24)**: The annual festival of the main shrine, Kono Jinja, and one of the most important observances for the entire sacred precinct, including Mana-i Jinja.

## History

Kasama Inari Jinja was founded, according to tradition, in 651 CE (the second year of Hakuchi). Shrine legend holds that this area was once open wilderness dominated by a single great walnut tree, and that local villagers, sensing the divine presence there, enshrined Ukanomitama no Kami — giving the shrine its old nickname "Kurumigashita Inari" ("Inari beneath the walnut tree"). For more than 1,360 years since, it has been venerated as a deity of bountiful harvests, business prosperity, and household safety.

During the Edo period the shrine received generous patronage from successive lords of the Kasama domain, beginning with Inoue Masakata, whose devotion spread the shrine's fame well beyond the domain's borders. After the Makino clan took over as lords of Kasama in 1746, the imperial court granted the shrine the title "Shoichii Inari Daimyojin," the highest rank in the Shinto hierarchy. The current main hall, built around 1860 in the shrine's late-Edo reconstruction, is now a National Important Cultural Property. Together with Fushimi Inari Taisha in Kyoto and Yutoku Inari Jinja in Saga, it is counted as one of Japan's "Three Great Inari Shrines," drawing roughly 3.5 million visitors a year and ranking among the most-visited Inari shrines anywhere in the country.

## Highlights

The main hall is covered in intricate openwork carvings by master craftsmen of the late Edo period, their vivid multicolor detail rewarding a close look. The towering great torii gate at the entrance to the approach once collapsed from age but was rebuilt in 2016 with donations gathered from across Japan, repainted in a vermillion shade chosen to match Kasama's townscape. Two wisteria vines on the grounds, designated a prefectural natural monument, burst into fragrant purple bloom each year from early to mid-May, drawing visitors from across the region.

Each autumn the shrine hosts the "Kasama Chrysanthemum Festival," which grew out of a morning-glory exhibition in the Meiji era before chrysanthemum displays were added in 1908. Today, elaborate chrysanthemum dolls and giant blooms fill the grounds, making it one of the signature sights of autumn in Kasama.

## Visiting Tips

Visiting just the main hall takes about 30–45 minutes. The shrine is roughly a 20-minute walk from JR Kasama Station, or about 5 minutes by taxi. Goshuin stamps are available at the shrine office to the left of the main hall, with special limited-edition designs offered during the chrysanthemum festival. The grounds stay open until sunset, so an early-morning visit in the quiet air is also worth considering.

## Annual Festivals

- **Hatsuuma Festival (first Day of the Horse in February)**: One of the most important festivals for any Inari shrine, drawing large crowds who pray for business success and a bountiful harvest.
- **Grand Festival (April 9)**: The shrine's most important annual rite, featuring a portable-shrine procession and a range of Shinto ceremonies.
- **Rice Planting Festival (around June)**: A ceremonial planting held in the shrine's sacred rice paddy to pray for a bountiful harvest.
- **Kasama Chrysanthemum Festival (mid-October through late November)**: The grounds fill with giant chrysanthemum blooms and elaborate chrysanthemum dolls, a signature sight of autumn in Kasama.
- **Year-End Market and Purification Rite (December and February)**: Traditional observances marking the close of the year and the warding-off of misfortune.

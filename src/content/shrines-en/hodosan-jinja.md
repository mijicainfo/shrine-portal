## History

Hodosan Jinja stands at the southern foot of Mount Hodo (497m), at the northern edge of the Chichibu basin. According to shrine tradition, in the 41st year of Emperor Keiko's reign (111 CE), the legendary prince Yamato Takeru passed through this area on his way home from a campaign to pacify Japan's eastern provinces. Drawn to the mountain's unspoiled beauty, he set out to climb it, but was suddenly cut off by a raging wildfire partway up, with no way forward or back. At that moment, a mountain dog appeared out of nowhere, leapt into the flames to extinguish them, and guided Yamato Takeru safely to the summit before vanishing. Recognizing the dog as a messenger (goshinshi) of Oyamatsumi-no-Kami, the mountain god, Yamato Takeru gave thanks for the gods' protection and named the mountain "Hodo" — written with characters meaning "fire-stopping" — before enshrining three deities here: Kamiyamato-Iwarehiko-no-Mikoto (posthumously venerated as Emperor Jimmu, Japan's legendary first emperor and an ancestral deity of the imperial line), Oyamatsumi-no-Kami, the mountain god, and Homusubi-no-Kami, the god of fire. This is traditionally regarded as the founding of Hodosan Jinja.

Since then, Hodosan Jinja has been venerated for centuries as one of the "Chichibu Sanja," the three great shrines of the Chichibu region, alongside Chichibu Jinja and Mitsumine Jinja. Mitsumine Jinja is likewise associated with a wolf-messenger legend tied to Yamato Takeru's eastern campaign, but the wolf veneration at the two shrines grew out of the same regional tradition of mountain asceticism (shugendo) in Chichibu as independent traditions in their own right — no record survives of a direct branch relationship or shared lineage between the two. From the Edo period onward, Hodosan Jinja became known for its protective power against fire and theft; in the modern era, Eiichi Shibusawa — the industrialist later called "the father of Japanese capitalism," born in neighboring Fukaya and a driving force behind developing Nagatoro as a tourist destination — was a frequent visitor, and the shrine's goshuin today still carries a stamp referencing "Seien," his pen name.

## Highlights

Pairs of mountain-dog statues (the shrine's guardian messengers, known locally as Okuchi-no-Magami or simply "Oinu-sama") stand throughout the grounds in the position usually reserved for komainu lion-dogs, a living reminder of the dog that is said to have saved Yamato Takeru — and they remain objects of devotion for protection against fire and theft. The shrine buildings, built in the ornate gongen-zukuri style with a honden, heiden, and haiden, are covered in vividly colored carvings considered some of the finest in the region.

Behind the main shrine, the Okumiya (inner shrine) near the summit of Mount Hodo is easily reached by the Hodosan Ropeway, a roughly 5-minute, 832-meter ride from a station beside the shrine. The summit area gathers the quiet Okumiya — guarded by its own pair of mountain-dog statues — alongside the Ume Hyakkaen plum garden (about 170 varieties and 470 trees, best viewed early February through late March), a wintersweet (robai) grove of around 3,000 trees that perfumes the mountainside from late December through late February, a small animal park with rabbits, monkeys, llamas and more, and the SUSABINO Terrace café with its mountain views — together making for a full day's visit. Some visitors skip the ropeway and reach the Okumiya on foot via a hiking trail, a round trip of roughly two hours.

## Visiting Tips

After paying your respects at the worship hall, take time to admire the mountain-dog statues and the shrine's carvings up close. The shrine office, where goshuin and amulets are available, keeps hours of roughly 8:30–17:00 from April through September and 8:30–16:30 from October through March, so plan your visit with time to spare. The free parking lot in front of the shrine has limited capacity and tends to fill up by midday during the February wintersweet-and-plum season or on weekends, so arriving early or using the paid lot near the ropeway's base station is a safer bet. If you plan to visit the Okumiya, check the ropeway's operating hours (roughly 9:30 or 9:40 to 17:00) in advance and budget extra time for the summit.

## Annual Festivals

- **Wintersweet Festival (late December–late February)**: The roughly 3,000 wintersweet (robai) trees atop Mount Hodo bloom, filling the mountainside with their sweet winter fragrance.
- **Plum Festival (early February–late March)**: The summit's Ume Hyakkaen garden bursts into bloom with about 170 varieties and 470 plum trees, painting the slope red and white.
- **Spring Grand Festival (April 3)**: The shrine's most important annual festival, with rites beginning at 10:00 a.m. followed by a performance of sacred Hodosan kagura dance.
- **Okumiya Festival (May 2)**: A rite held at the Okumiya near the summit.
- **Funadama Festival (August 15)**: A Nagatoro-specific festival praying for safety on the waters of the Arakawa River.

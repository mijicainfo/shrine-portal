## History

Izumo Daijingu stands at the foot of Mt. Mikage, in the eastern part of the Kameoka Basin. The mountain itself is believed to have been worshipped since ancient times as a kannabi — a mountain dwelling place of the kami — and is thought to have been a site of worship even before any shrine buildings existed here. According to shrine tradition, the current shrine buildings were established on October 21, 709, though the mountain's sacred status likely predates that date by a considerable margin.

The shrine enshrines two deities: Okuninushi no Mikoto and his consort, Mihotsuhime no Mikoto. Listed in the Engishiki as a myojin-taisha (major shrine), Izumo Daijingu served as the ichinomiya, or chief shrine, of the former Tanba Province, and held the rank of kokuhei-chusha (a nationally supported shrine) under the pre-1945 system of shrine ranks. Today it operates as an independent shrine, unaffiliated with the Association of Shinto Shrines (Jinja Honcho).

Izumo Daijingu is also widely known by the nickname "Moto-Izumo," meaning "original Izumo." This name comes from a tradition holding that the deity now enshrined at Izumo Taisha in Shimane Prefecture was originally enshrined here, before being transferred west — and it's said that before the Edo period, the name "Izumo" itself sometimes referred to this shrine in Tanba rather than the famous one in Shimane. It's worth being clear, though, that this is a tradition passed down at Izumo Daijingu rather than an established fact accepted by historians generally; Izumo Taisha is far better known nationally, and its own history is understood differently. Visitors curious about the two shrines' relationship are best served by treating the "Moto-Izumo" story as local shrine tradition — a fascinating point of local pride, but a legend rather than settled history.

The present honden (main hall), said to have been rebuilt in 1345 under the direction of the shogun Ashikaga Takauji, is a three-bay nagare-zukuri structure with a cypress-bark roof, and is designated a National Important Cultural Property.

## Highlights

Rising behind the honden, Mt. Mikage is considered the shrine's goshintai — its sacred body itself. Several iwakura (sacred rocks) are scattered across its slopes, including a cluster said to enshrine Kunitokotachi no Kami, reachable via a trail up the mountain. Entry requires borrowing a sash from the shrine office rather than wandering in freely. The mountain's atmosphere has earned Izumo Daijingu a reputation in popular and spiritual media as one of Kyoto's foremost "power spots" — a widely repeated claim worth taking as folklore and popular reputation rather than verified fact, but part of what draws many visitors here.

Within the grounds is a spring known as Manai no Mizu, fed by water filtering down through Mt. Mikage. Long cherished as a source of pure water, it flows from beneath a large, rope-bound sacred rock and is one of the shrine's most photographed features; some visitors take the water home with them.

The honden, rebuilt in 1345 under Ashikaga Takauji, is a dignified three-bay nagare-zukuri structure with a cypress-bark roof, designated a National Important Cultural Property. Set against the deep green of Mt. Mikage, it carries the quiet dignity befitting the chief shrine of a former province.

## Visiting Tips

The grounds can be visited at any time, but the shrine office — for amulets and goshuin — is open 9:00–17:00, with goshuin reception until 16:30. Visitors wishing to climb toward the iwakura on Mt. Mikage should stop by the shrine office first to borrow the required sash.

The community bus connecting the shrine to the nearest train stations runs on a limited schedule, so it's worth checking the timetable in advance. Drivers can use one of the three free parking areas near the shrine (roughly 80 spaces combined), though the shrine becomes very crowded around its April and October festivals, when public transport is the more reliable option.

## Annual Festivals

- **Kayaura Shinji (January 15)**: A traditional divination rite performed to foretell the year's harvest.
- **Spring Festival (April 18)**: The shrine's spring festival features the Izumo Furyu Hana Odori, a flower dance registered as an intangible folk cultural property of Kyoto Prefecture.
- **Autumn Grand Festival (October 21)**: Held on the traditional anniversary of the shrine's founding, this is Izumo Daijingu's most important festival, with a mikoshi procession through the surrounding Chitose and Maji districts.
- **Niiname-sai (November 23)**: An autumn festival of thanksgiving for the year's harvest.

## History

Sanno Shrine stands in Sakamoto, Nagasaki City. Its name is read "Sanno-jinja" according to the reading on Nagasaki City's cultural property page. The official site says it is also called "Sanno Hiyoshi Shrine" (山王日吉神社) and "Urakami Kotai Jingu" (浦上皇大神宮), and explains that it was formed by merging Sanno Shrine (Hiyoshi Shrine), a village shrine, with Kotai Jingu, a prefectural shrine (with the former enshrined jointly on the Kotai Jingu side).

According to the official history, it began with the establishment of a Sanno Gongen shrine in 1638 (Kan'ei 15). Matsudaira Izu-no-kami Nobutsuna, passing through this area on his way back from the Shimabara Rebellion, is said to have proposed inviting the Sanno Gongen deity here because the scenery resembled the foot of Mount Hiei in Omi and the place was likewise called "Sakamoto," and the Nagasaki deputy Suetsugu Heizo (末次平蔵茂房), in consultation with the Nagasaki magistrate, built a shrine. The history also records that its first site was near the present one, or, in another account, around Okamachi. It moved to its present location in 1652 (Joo 1), and in 1868 (Meiji 1) it was renamed Hiyoshi Shrine and became a village shrine.

Meanwhile, in Meiji 1, Sawa Nobuyoshi, governor-general of the Nagasaki Court, pursued the founding of Urakami Kotai Jingu against the background of incidents involving the Christians of Urakami and other matters, and its buildings were completed in the third month of the following year on the site of today's Yamazato Elementary School. Kotai Jingu was badly damaged by a typhoon in August of Meiji 7, and after a joint grand festival in Meiji 15, permission to merge was obtained in December of Meiji 16. The transfer ceremony was held on the eighth day of the first month of Meiji 17, after which the shrine came to be called the prefectural shrine Urakami Kotai Jingu, according to the official history.

## Highlights

At 11:02 AM on August 9, 1945, an atomic bomb was dropped on Nagasaki. Sanno Shrine, about 800 m from the hypocenter, suffered severe damage; the official history states that everything collapsed in the bombing (原爆にて總て倒壊) and that the main sanctuary was rebuilt immediately.

**The one-legged torii (second torii)** is a torii installed in October 1924 (Taisho 13) when the approach was extended. The blast knocked down the pillar on the hypocenter side, leaving it standing on the other pillar alone. According to the Nagasaki Atomic Bomb Museum's map of A-bomb remains, the remaining top stone (kasaishi) was twisted by about 12 degrees, and on the hypocenter side of the standing pillar the heat rays melted minerals so that the carved inscriptions can no longer be read. According to the official site, of the four torii on the approach, the first and second, which stood parallel to the blast, remained, but the first torii fell in a traffic accident in 1962 (Showa 37), and only the second still stands as it did at the time. The base of the fallen pillar remains right beside it. This torii, together with the hypocenter and the former Shiroyama National School building among others, makes up the National Historic Site "Nagasaki Atomic Bomb Remains" (長崎原爆遺跡), designated on October 3, 2016. Note also that the official history says of the approach newly laid out in 1924 that "today's one-legged torii street became a city road after the war."

**The bombed camphor trees** are two great camphors at the entrance to the grounds, designated a Nagasaki City Natural Monument on February 15, 1969 (Showa 44). The city's cultural property page gives a girth at chest height of 8.20 m for the right-hand tree and about 6 m for the left, and says they form a great crown spreading 40 m east to west and 25 m north to south. Sources differ on their age — the official site says 500 to 600 years, the museum map over 400 years — and on their height — about 10 m according to the city's cultural property page, about 21 to 22 m according to the Nagasaki Kusunoki Project. In the bombing the upper main trunks broke and the heat rays scorched the bark, leaving them almost like dead trees, but they sprouted again. As for when, the museum map and the official site's fundraising page say about two months later, while the official site's page on the bombing says about two years later.

According to the Nagasaki Kusunoki Project, because the third torii collapsed in the bombing, a sacred shimenawa rope is tied to the camphors so that they also serve in place of a torii separating the inside of the shrine from the outside. According to the official site, the sound of wind in the leaves was selected for the Ministry of the Environment's "100 Soundscapes of Japan to Be Preserved." The city newsletter reports that a 2018 cultural property survey found other bombed camphors as well, revealing a group of 19 camphor trees. The museum map notes that this tree is also known because Nagasaki-born Masaharu Fukuyama (<a href="https://www.instagram.com/masaharu_fukuyama_official/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">official Instagram</a>, <a href="https://x.com/BROS_1991" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">official X</a>) released the song "クスノキ" (Kusunoki), whose motif is this tree.

## Visiting Tips

This is a place that conveys the memory of the atomic bombing, and it is also a shrine. Nagasaki City's newsletter asks visitors to worship while respecting its quiet atmosphere. Regular visiting hours are not stated on the official site.

Amulets and talismans are offered at any time at the amulet counter on the grounds or at the shrine office. They include the "Okusu-mamori" (大くす守), which draws on the vitality of the great camphors, and the "Futo Fukutsu" amulet (不倒不屈の御守), inspired by the second torii. The "Tanuki Tenugui" fundraising campaign (タヌキ手ぬぐい募金) of the Sanno Shrine Great Camphor Preservation Association (山王神社大楠を守る会), which protects the bombed camphors, is also accepted on the grounds. Those wishing to request prayers such as exorcism of misfortune or safe childbirth are asked to contact the shrine in advance.

There is no parking, so use public transport or a nearby parking lot.

## Annual Festivals

- **Getsurei-sai (1st of each month) and Saitan-sai (New Year's Day)**: Listed in the official annual events.
- **Setsubun-sai and Koshinsatsu Shono-sai, the burning of old talismans (February 3)**: In 2026, the shrine announced a lucky-bean and lucky-mochi throwing on the stage in the grounds (advance booking by phone, first 30 people) and an onibi bonfire.
- **Koyairi of the dance towns (June 1)**: The event regarded as the start of rehearsals for the neighborhoods that offer dances at the autumn festival.
- **Nagoshi-sai (June 30)**: The summer purification. In 2026 the shrine announced that a chinowa reed ring would be set up.
- **Chinju no Mori Cafe (August)**: For 2026, the shrine announced that it would be held on the grounds on August 8 and 9, with a silent prayer under the bombed camphors planned for 11:02 on the 9th.
- **Shuki Taisai, Urakami Kunchi (October 17)**: The 2026 notice (cancelled in case of rain) listed Shinto rites, mikoshi, a children's procession and dedicatory dances, with the mikoshi's resting place (otabisho) in Hamaguchi-machi.
- **Niiname-sai (November 23)**: A festival of thanksgiving for the new harvest.
- **Oharae-shiki and Joya-sai (December)**: The official site lists the Oharae-shiki under December 23, while its description of the Joya-sai says it is held on New Year's Eve following the Oharae-shiki. Check the date in advance.

Details change from year to year, so check the official site's news for the latest.

## History

Sumidagawa Jinja stands in Tsutsumidori, Sumida Ward, Tokyo. In older times it was called Suijin-gu or Ukishima no Miya, and it is affectionately known as "Suijin-sama," the water god (Tokyo Shrine Association). The enshrined deities are Hayaakitsuhiko no Kami, Hayaakitsuhime no Kami, Tori no Iwakusufune no Kami and Okaji Kidohime no Kami.

According to the Tokyo Shrine Association's history, Minamoto no Yoritomo is said to have prayed at the shrine when he met a storm while coming down to the Kanto region in the Jisho era, but the year of its founding is unknown. It is described as the local guardian shrine of Sumida, widely and deeply revered by boatmen and cargo-boat crews, and it was renamed "Sumidagawa Jinja" in 1872.

According to an explanation by the Museum of Maritime Science (Fune no Kagakukan), the site was once the end point of the Sumida River, covered in dense woods, which is why it was called "Suijin no Mori" (the Water God's Forest), and because it did not flood even when the river rose it was also known as "Ukishima no Miya" (floating-island shrine). Utagawa Hiroshige drew "Sumidagawa Suijin no Mori Massaki" in his One Hundred Famous Views of Edo, and the explanation notes that the shrine's torii can be seen among the pine trees. The guardian forest on the riverbank was moved about 100 meters by postwar development, and the shrine now stands at its present site.

TV Tokyo's "Shutsubotsu! Ad-machic Tengoku" featured the shrine, together with Sumida Inari Shrine, at No. 3 ("Two Reitaisai") in its Kanegafuchi episode broadcast on August 23, 2025 (according to the program's official page). The page explains that every June, the annual festivals of Sumida Inari Shrine to the east of Kanegafuchi Station and Sumidagawa Shrine to the west are held. Please note that this is a single entry covering the two shrines, not a ranking for Sumidagawa Jinja alone.

## Highlights

The main highlight is the shrine building, designated by Sumida Ward. According to the ward's official page, it is a Gongen-style complex of main hall, offering hall and worship hall, rebuilt after the earlier hall (rebuilt in 1848) collapsed in the Ansei Edo earthquake. Ridgepole tablets show that the worship hall was built in 1858 and the main and offering halls in 1864. The roof ornaments such as gables, along with carvings on the frog-leg struts and rainbow beams and the carved side panels, reflect late-Edo to Bakumatsu styles, and as a rare Edo-period shrine building in the ward it became a Registered Tangible Cultural Property on August 27, 2020 and a designated Tangible Cultural Property on October 24, 2024.

The shrine is located within Higashi-Shirahige Park (according to the Japanese Wikipedia and a personal blog). The Sumida tourism association page notes a stone turtle and a ship's anchor on the grounds, and that Sumida-bayashi festival music is practiced around the kagura-den (sacred dance hall). Some guide sites say that the guardian statues in front of the worship hall are turtles rather than the usual lion-dogs, called "koma-game" (information from personal and guide sites).

## Visiting Tips

The shrine is about a 10-minute walk from Kanegafuchi Station on the Tobu Isesaki Line (Tokyo Shrine Association; the Sumida tourism association page says 7 minutes). Visiting hours and shrine-office hours are not given on those pages, and an official shrine website could not be found. The phone number is 03-3611-3089.

According to a personal blog (Jinja Memo), the goshuin costs ¥300 and is said to be a pre-written sheet received at the shrine office. The shrine's own notice could not be confirmed, so calling ahead is the safest. Parking availability and fees could not be confirmed from official information either, so public transport is recommended.

## Annual Festivals

- **Annual festival (June 15)**: The Tokyo Shrine Association lists the festival day as June 15. According to the Museum of Maritime Science, a portable-shrine procession is held at the festival. The Sumida tourism association's event listing shows the 2024 festival on June 15–16. Dates may change by year, so check with the shrine for the latest information.

Other annual events are not listed because no verifiable information could be found.

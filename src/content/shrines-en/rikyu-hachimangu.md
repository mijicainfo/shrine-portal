## History

Rikyu Hachimangu stands in Oyamazaki, Otokuni District, Kyoto Prefecture, right beside JR Yamazaki Station. Its reading is given as "Rikyu Hachimangu" in Japanese Wikipedia and elsewhere (the official site does not appear to give a reading). It is a different shrine from Iwashimizu Hachimangu in Yawata. According to the official site, the main sanctuary behind the worship hall enshrines Emperor Ojin, the left sanctuary Sakatoke no Okami (also called Oyamatsumi no Kami), and the right sanctuary the three Hime deities.

According to the official history, at the start of the Heian period Emperor Seiwa dreamed that the sun entered his body and received a divine message to bring the deity Hachiman from Usa Hachimangu in Kyushu to the capital. The monk Gyokyo, ordered by the emperor to carry out the task, saw a spiritual light on a mountain at night (Kamioriyama) at the port of Yamazaki on his return. When he dug a little at the spot, clear water welled up between the rocks, so he enshrined the sacred object there and, in 859 (Jogan 1), built the shrine. The site was the former Kayo-no-miya, a detached palace of Emperor Saga, and the shrine's name was later changed to "Rikyu Hachimangu."

The shrine is also known for its link with oil. The official site explains that in the Jogan era a shrine priest of the time received a divine message, invented a pressing tool called the "nagaki," and began making egoma (perilla) oil. It was first offered as lamp oil to shrines and temples, then spread across the country, and Rikyu Hachimangu was given the title "Aburaso" (founder of oil) by the imperial court. As an oil guild (abura-za) it held a monopoly, and oil merchants from other provinces could not handle oil without the shrine's license. The Kyoto Prefecture tourism page also calls it the "birthplace of oil pressing in Japan."

Later, according to the official site, it once had shrine buildings so grand that it was called the "Nikko of the West," but it gradually declined because of Oda Nobunaga's open-market policies and the mass production of rapeseed oil. In the 1864 Kinmon Incident it became a garrison for the Choshu domain and was shelled, and its extensive shrine lands were burned to a wasteland. In 1876 the opening of the Tokaido Main Line between Kyoto and Kobe turned most of the grounds into railway land, the shrine buildings were rebuilt in 1879 through donations from devotees, and they were reconstructed in 1929, giving the shrine its present form.

## Highlights

The official site says the only buildings to survive the fighting and remain today are the "Somon" (main gate) and the "Higashimon" (east gate), both designated cultural properties of Oyamazaki Town. In the official walk-through list of the grounds, the Kashiki stone and the shrine-land boundary marker stone are also listed as Oyamazaki Town designated cultural properties. The Agency for Cultural Affairs' "Cultural Heritage Online" lists the main hall, worship hall, middle gate, see-through fence, water-purification pavilion, and the shrine 高天宮神社 as Registered Tangible Cultural Properties (buildings) of the Showa era.

The official list of the grounds also includes a monument marking the birthplace of oil pressing in Japan, a monument to the site of Kayo-no-miya, a statue of the founder of oil, a sign for oil dealers nationwide, and the Iwashimizu well. According to the Oyamazaki Chamber of Commerce tourism page, next to the main hall is the Tenjin Koshikake Stone, said to be where Sugawara no Michizane sat, and sitting on it is said to bring success in studies.

## Visiting Tips

Visiting hours are not stated on the official site. The Oyamazaki Chamber of Commerce tourism page gives 6:00 AM–5:00 PM and another guide site gives 9:00 AM–5:00 PM. Several guides describe entry to the grounds as free.

Goshuin are not mentioned on the official site. According to guide sites they are given out at the shrine office, and one gives the fee as ¥300 each (to be confirmed). As for amulets and other items, the official site's page on offerings (undated) lists an "Yudan Taiteki" (Never Let Your Guard Down) amulet, a Rikyu Hachimangu ema, a traffic-safety amulet, an academic-success amulet, and sacred oil (egoma oil). Prices may change, so check on site.

According to the official site, it is about a 1-minute walk from Yamazaki Station (JR Kyoto Line) and about 3 minutes from Oyamazaki Station (Hankyu Kyoto Line). The official site says parking is monthly-contract only, so going by train is the safe choice. The official site's event records mainly date from fiscal 2009, so it is wise to confirm events and hours by phone (075-956-0218).

## Annual Festivals

According to the official site's annual events and related pages, the following events are held. Dates may change from year to year, so check before you go.

- **Saitan-sai (New Year's Day)**: A year-end great purification begins at midnight, followed by the Saitan-sai ceremony at 11:00 AM.
- **Dondo-sai (Sagicho, January)**: An event in which New Year decorations and old amulets and talismans are burned.
- **Hinoto-sai (April)**: The spring festival, described as "the shrine's first and most important festival." It is modeled on the ritual of Hachiman's procession and includes a lamp-offering rite and a yudate (boiling-water) rite. The fiscal 2009 event record gives April 4, while Japanese Wikipedia gives April 3 (date to be confirmed).
- **Abura no Hi, Oil Day (August 23)**: A day to give thanks for the blessings of oil, tied to the transfer of the shrine on August 23 of Jogan 1. The official site says egoma oil is handed to worshippers that day (while supplies last; the official page carries no date).
- **Autumn festival, Hojo-e (September 15)**: A yudate rite is offered.
- **Shichi-Go-San (November)**: The official site announces reservations being accepted during November.
- **Toshikoshi-sai (December 31)**: Amazake and rice cakes are served (some for a fee).

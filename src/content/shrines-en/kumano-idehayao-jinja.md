## History

Kumano Idehayao Jinja stands at the summit of Mt. Minakami (659 meters), a mountain rising southeast of Matsushiro in Nagano City. Shrine tradition dates its founding to 718, during the Nara period, when Idehayao no Mikoto was first enshrined here; the shrine marked its 1,300th anniversary in 2018. Idehayao no Mikoto is traditionally regarded as a son of the great deity of Suwa (Takeminakata) and has long been worshipped as the ancestral pioneer who opened this region to settlement, as well as a god of agriculture.

From the medieval period onward, Mt. Minakami flourished as a major sacred site of Kumano-lineage Shugendo, the mountain ascetic tradition, after a divided spirit of the Kumano deities was invited here — giving rise to its name at the time, "Kumano Sansha Dai-Gongen." Mountain ascetics (yamabushi) undertook rigorous training on the slopes, and traces of that history remain in the area today. Following the Meiji-era edict separating Shinto and Buddhism, the ascetics were forced to return to secular life, and the shrine took its present name, Kumano Idehayao Jinja. Locally, the summit shrine is grouped together with subsidiary shrines on the grounds — including Jiju Jinja, which fronts the approach — under the collective name "Minakami Jinja."

Jiju Jinja enshrines Jiju Okami. According to tradition, this deity was born as the third son of Uchiyama Mitsuaki, lord of Uchiyama Castle in Saku, Shinano Province, and trained as a mountain ascetic on Mt. Minakami. At his death by self-mummification (nyujo) in 1556, he is said to have declared three times that he was a manifestation of Fudo Myoo. He is worshipped as a guardian of child-rearing, longevity, and protection from fire, and local tradition holds that a child apprenticed to him will grow up free of illness — a belief that still brings families to the shrine soon after a birth.

The current main hall is said to have been rebuilt in 1389 and was designated a Nagano Prefectural cultural property on August 15, 1994, a rare surviving structure connected to the mountain's history of Shugendo practice.

## Highlights

Mt. Minakami's most distinctive feature is its unusually cone-shaped profile, which stands apart from the surrounding peaks. In the 1930s, this led Sakai Katsutoki — a Christian evangelist and occultist — to claim that ancient Japan had built artificial pyramids, with Mt. Minakami as one of them. His book on the subject was banned by the authorities of the era, and the theory has never been substantiated by mainstream scholarship; it survives today purely as a well-known, unverified legend, and signage on the mountain describing the enshrined deities in similarly speculative terms should be read the same way. Geological boring surveys tell a different story: Mt. Minakami is a natural andesite lava dome, formed by viscous lava that piled up near a vent roughly 300,000 to 350,000 years ago, with a lava layer some 150 meters thick — and no physical evidence of artificial construction. Partway up the slope sits Iwato Jinja, popularly rumored to be the pyramid's "entrance." It has no shrine building to speak of — only a rock cave with a sacred mirror enshrined inside — a simple, striking form of worship in its own right.

The mountain's other defining event is real and well documented: the Matsushiro earthquake swarm, which began on August 3, 1965, and continued for roughly five and a half years. Its epicenters clustered directly beneath Mt. Minakami. More than 711,000 tremors were recorded, over 62,000 of them felt by residents. At the peak of the activity, the mountain itself rose by roughly one meter, an estimated 10 million cubic meters of groundwater surged to the surface, and a landslide in the Makiuchi district to the south destroyed 11 homes. The scientific consensus attributes the swarm to a "water-driven" mechanism — high-pressure groundwater fracturing the bedrock — rather than volcanic activity, and it remains a significant case study in seismology today. Unlike the pyramid legend, this is an observed and measured geological event.

## Visiting Tips

The grounds can be visited at any hour, but there is no priest stationed at the shrine office day to day, and goshuin are issued only as pre-written (kaki-oki) copies. A paved road leads most of the way up the mountain, with visitor parking beside the Zuishinmon gate near the summit — but the road is narrow, so walking up is not especially recommended, and drivers should watch carefully for oncoming traffic. The grounds themselves are modest in size, but the quiet mountaintop atmosphere and the view over the Matsushiro and Kawanakajima plains are well worth the trip.

## Annual Festivals

- **Nekooroshi Festival (January 4)**: A large shimenawa made from new straw and a wooden board bearing New Year's wishes are set up at the shrine entrance, alongside rice-cake ornaments shaped like ripened ears of rice and miniature farming tools carved from sumac wood, all offered in prayer for a bountiful harvest.
- **Hatsuho Festival, known as the Wheat Festival (July 28)**: Harvested wheat is purified with a mortar and pestle "seven and a half times" the night before, then steamed over sacred fire the next day and offered to the deity together with raw wheat, in thanks for a successful harvest.
- **Nekomaki Festival (December 23)**: Marking the close of the year's cycle of harvest prayers begun on January 4, a rice-cake "neko" is offered in a closing ceremony of thanks for the year's agricultural abundance.
- **Mt. Minakami Pyramid Festival (May 5)**: Organized by a local neighborhood committee rather than the shrine itself, this community event celebrates Mt. Minakami's "world's largest and oldest pyramid" legend every year on May 5 (Children's Day), running from around 10:00 a.m. to 1:00 p.m.

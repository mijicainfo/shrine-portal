## History

Kono Jinja stands at the base of the Amanohashidate sandbar in Miyazu, Kyoto Prefecture, and holds the rank of Ichinomiya, the first shrine, of former Tango Province. Shrine tradition holds that, in ancient times, Toyouke-Omikami was enshrined at Mana-i-hara, the site of today's okunomiya Mana-i Jinja, and that Amaterasu-Omikami later came to be enshrined there as well. In time, Amaterasu-Omikami journeyed on to what is now the Inner Shrine of Ise Jingu, and Toyouke-Omikami to Ise's Outer Shrine. Because this was the place where the deities resided before their final enshrinement at Ise, Kono Jinja and Mana-i Jinja came to be known as "Moto-Ise," shrines directly tied to the origin story of Ise Jingu itself.

After the deities' departure, a shrine continued to be maintained at Mana-i-hara, but in 671 its name is said to have been changed to "Kono-miya," and in 719 a shrine hall was built and the shrine relocated to its present site. The 927 Engishiki shrine registry records it as a Myojin Taisha, a rank entitled to receive imperial offerings at the Tsukinami and Niiname festivals — the only shrine among the eight provinces of the Sanindo region (Tango, Tajima, Tanba, Inaba, Hoki, Oki, Izumo, and Iwami) to hold this distinction, cementing its standing as Ichinomiya of Tango. Under the modern shrine ranking system it was a Kokuhei Chusha (national middle shrine), and it is counted today among the Jinja Honcho's Beppyo Jinja.

The shrine's hereditary priestly family, the Amabe clan, traces its lineage to Hikohoakari-no-Mikoto himself. The Amabe Clan Genealogy they preserve is recognized as Japan's oldest surviving genealogical record and designated a National Treasure, standing as first-rate historical evidence of Kono Jinja's deep ties to the Imperial family and to Ise Jingu.

## Highlights

Kono Jinja's greatest attraction is the view of Amanohashidate stretching out directly in front of its grounds. One of Japan's "Three Views," this pine-covered sandbar appears to rise into the sky like an ascending dragon when viewed upside-down, between one's legs, from Amanohashidate View Land to the south — a perspective known as "Hiryukan," the "flying dragon view" (the view from Kasamatsu Park to the north is called "Shoryukan," or "ascending dragon view"). Kono Jinja occupies a prime position directly overlooking this famous scenery.

The shrine's architecture is also noteworthy. Its main hall follows the Shinmei-zukuri style modeled on the Inner Shrine of Ise Jingu, with a cypress-bark roof and ornamental spheres called "goshiki no suetama" (five-colored jewels) set along its gable ends — a feature said to be shared by only Ise Jingu and Kono Jinja, reflecting the deep bond between the two shrines. The present shrine hall was rebuilt in 1845.

Among the shrine's treasures, alongside the National Treasure Amabe Clan Genealogy, are a pair of ancient bronze mirrors — the Hentsu-kagami, from the Former Han dynasty, and the Okitsu-kagami, from the Later Han dynasty — both designated Important Cultural Properties and unveiled publicly in 1987 after roughly 2,000 years in the Amabe family's keeping; the genealogy itself records their transmission. A pair of Kamakura-period komainu guardian lion-dogs along the approach is also designated an Important Cultural Property, and legend holds that the statues once roamed at night as far as Amanohashidate, until they were struck with a sword to subdue them — said to explain markings on the statues resembling sword cuts.

## Visiting Tips

The grounds are open 8:00–17:00 from February to November, and 8:00–16:30 from December to January (hours may be extended on weekends and holidays). The shrine office, where goshuin, talismans, and amulets are available, is open from 8:30 until closing, though goshuin themselves are issued only from 9:00 — worth keeping in mind for an early-morning visit.

Most visitors pay their respects at Kono Jinja first, then continue up the roughly 10-minute path (about 550m) behind the main hall to the okunomiya, Mana-i Jinja. Mana-i Jinja has almost no shrine office of its own, so its goshuin is normally received at Kono Jinja's shrine office. A parking area for worshippers is available on the grounds: the first 30 minutes are free, after which a preservation fee of ¥800 for the day applies to a car (revised August 1, 2025). From Amanohashidate Station, options include walking the pine-lined sandbar, renting a bicycle, taking a sightseeing boat, or riding the local bus — choose whichever suits the time you have.

## Annual Festivals

- **Aoi Matsuri (Annual Festival, April 24)**: Said to carry over 2,500 years of history, this is among the oldest annual festivals in the Tango region. Its highlight is a layered program of ritual performances offered by different local districts — lion dances, tachifuri sword dances, sacred kagura music, and bamboo flute (sasabayashi) music — and it is designated a Kyoto Prefecture Intangible Folk Cultural Property.
- **Summer Grand Purification (June 30)**: A ritual to cleanse the sins and impurities accumulated over the first half of the year, including passage through a great chinowa ring of woven grass.

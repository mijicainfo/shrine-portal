## History

Nakayama Jinja has long been venerated as the ichinomiya, or highest-ranked shrine, of former Mimasaka Province. Tradition places its founding in 707, though another well-supported theory holds that it was established around 713, when Mimasaka Province was carved out of six districts of Bizen Province, with a spirit invited here from Kibi-no-Nakayama in what is now Kita Ward, Okayama City. The shrine's earliest confirmed appearance in the historical record comes in the Nihon Sandai Jitsuroku in 860, and the Engishiki shrine register of 927 lists it as the sole Myojin Taisha (great shrine) in all of Mimasaka Province — a status it held onto as the province's ichinomiya for centuries afterward. In the medieval period, it appears in the Konjaku Monogatarishu as the home of a monkey deity, and it is named alongside Itsukushima Shrine in Aki and Kibitsu Shrine in Bitchu in the Ryojin Hisho, an anthology compiled under the retired emperor Go-Shirakawa — testimony to how widely known the shrine was at the time.

The primary deity is Kagamitsukuri no Kami ("the mirror-making deity"), enshrined together with Amenonukado no Kami and Ishikoridome no Kami. Ishikoridome no Kami is the deity credited in Japanese mythology with casting the sacred mirror (yata no kagami) used to help lure the sun goddess Amaterasu from the heavenly cave, linking this trio of deities closely with the casting and working of metal. When Nakayama Jinja was ranked a Kokuhei-chusha, a mid-level state-supported shrine, under the Meiji-era shrine ranking system in 1871, its enshrined deity was for a time officially registered as Kanayamahiko no Mikoto, a god associated with mining and metalworking, before further research led the shrine to revert to Kagamitsukuri no Kami as its principal deity after World War II. The Mimasaka region was historically known for tatara ironworking in the mountains of the Chugoku range, and Nakayama Jinja's long association with metal-related deities is often discussed alongside that regional history of mining and iron production.

## Highlights

The single greatest highlight at Nakayama Jinja is its honden (main hall), rebuilt in 1559 with a donation from the deputy military governor Amago Haruhisa. Its three-by-three-bay irimoya structure, entered from the gable end and fronted by a one-bay curved karahafu canopy, is known as "Nakayama-zukuri," a style found nowhere else in Japan outside this immediate area, and was designated a National Important Cultural Property in 1914. Among single shrine buildings nationwide, it ranks among the largest, and its broad cypress-bark roof gives it a dignified, imposing presence well worth seeing up close. The worship hall and offering hall, rebuilt in 1922 to coincide with repairs to the honden, share the same cypress-bark-roofed irimoya construction; valued for their curved karahafu canopy and coffered ceilings nearly as highly as the honden itself, they are separately listed on the national Register of Tangible Cultural Properties.

The shrine gate is a four-legged yakuimon-style gate relocated in the early Meiji era from the second bailey of Tsuyama Castle, and is itself designated a Tsuyama City cultural property. Rather than the usual pair of komainu lion-dogs, the grounds are watched over by statues of monkeys, said to recall a legend from the Konjaku Monogatarishu about the subjugation of a monkey deity. Several subsidiary shrines, including Saru-jinja (the "monkey shrine"), Soshinden, Kunishisha, and Misakisha, cluster within the grounds, reflecting the many layers of history behind Mimasaka's ichinomiya. Nakayama-zukuri itself went on to shape the region's shrine architecture more broadly, adopted for the honden of Mimasaka Soja-gu and Tsuruyama Hachiman-gu — both National Important Cultural Properties in Tsuyama — as well as Takano Jinja and Tokumori Jinja nearby.

## Visiting Tips

The grounds can generally be visited 8:30–17:00, with prayer (kito) requests accepted 8:00–16:00. Goshuin are available for ¥500, hand-written to order at the shrine office between 9:00 and 17:00; since the priest sometimes serves other shrines and may be away, it's worth calling ahead (0868-27-0051) if receiving one is important to your visit. The shrine sits in a quiet spot along the old Sakushu Kaido road, about 5–6 km north of central Tsuyama, and free parking for roughly 500 cars makes arriving by car the most convenient option. Those relying on public transport can take the Chutetsu Bus bound for Nishitabe from JR Tsuyama Station and get off at the Ichinomiya stop. Because the honden, worship hall, and offering hall are all nationally designated or registered cultural properties, please avoid touching the buildings when viewing them up close.

## Annual Festivals

- **Otaue Matsuri, Spring Grand Festival (April 29)**: A rite praying for a bountiful harvest. Around 10:30 a.m., following a ceremony in the main hall, a dozen or so parishioners dressed in white perform the motions of hoeing and planting seedlings across the shrine's stone-paved grounds, symbolically treated as a rice paddy, while a pair of male and female shishi lions dance to flute and drum music. This long-transmitted agricultural rite draws large numbers of visitors from within and beyond the prefecture each year.
- **Shinkosai, Autumn Grand Festival (November 3)**: An autumn festival centered on a sacred palanquin procession through the surrounding parish, one of the shrine's largest annual events, supported by the whole local community.
- **Oshimesai (December)**: A year-end rite said to originate in prayers offered at shrines and temples nationwide against the Mongol invasions of the Kamakura period.

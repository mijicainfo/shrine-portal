## History

According to shrine tradition, Hikotake-gu's origins trace back to 89 CE, the 18th year of the reign of Emperor Keiko, the 12th emperor. When Kumatsuhiko rose in rebellion in the province of Hyuga (present-day Miyazaki), the emperor personally led an army that landed in Kyushu and made camp at Yurugidake in Yamashiro district. There he prayed for victory, and it is said that divine power surged from the peak of Mt. Hikotake to Yurugidake, shaking the mountain so violently that the rebel forces fled at once. After subduing Kumatsuhiko and his ally, the earth-spider chieftain Tsutsura, the emperor, on his way home, built three shrines on the peak of Mt. Hikotake — the Upper, Middle, and Lower Shrines — in gratitude for the gods' help. This is said to be the founding of Hikotake-gu, which has been worshipped in Yamaga for more than 1,900 years since.

The shrine has seen its share of decline and revival over the centuries. In 1579, the Ryuzoji clan of Hizen burned down the shrine buildings while returning from an attack on the allied Akahoshi clan, and in 1587 the shrine lost its lands under Toyotomi Hideyoshi's pacification of Kyushu. Kato Kiyomasa, who later became lord of the Higo domain, donated shrine lands and support for the priesthood, and through the efforts of the Yoshida family of priests, the shrine buildings were rebuilt in 1671. The Yoshida family has served as Hikotake-gu's hereditary priests ever since, a line that continues down to the present day.

## The eraser-stamp goshuin: one priest's challenge

In recent years, Hikotake-gu has drawn wide attention for its "eraser stamp" (keshigomu-hanko) goshuin, hand-carved by the head priest, Mitsuhiro Yoshida. About five years ago, Yoshida left a company job to take over the shrine from his aging father. At the time, Hikotake-gu was drawing only 20 to 30 visitors a month, and the shrine alone could not support him — he reportedly worked night shifts at a factory while serving as priest by day.

"Whether this becomes a shrine no one visits, or one that many people come to, depends on what I do from here," Yoshida has said of his thinking at the time. With no prior training, he taught himself to carve eraser stamps, working the shrine's komainu guardian lion-dogs, seasonal scenery from the grounds, and local wildlife into his designs. Each hand-carved, monthly-rotating design brought something new for repeat visitors to discover, and word spread through social media and the shrine's online shop. The result was a roughly 100-fold increase in the number of people visiting Hikotake-gu.

This story — "the challenge of a priest protecting a shrine with 1,900 years of history" — was featured on an RKK Kumamoto Broadcasting news segment on September 18, 2026, under the theme of "unearthing a dormant appeal," and was syndicated by Yahoo! News Japan the same day. A centuries-old shrine finding new visitors through one priest's handmade, present-day idea has become a widely cited example of how a small rural shrine can confront the challenges of succession and depopulation head-on.

## Highlights

Hikotake-gu is a shrine deeply rooted in mountain worship, with its Lower, Middle, and Upper shrines set in stages from the foot of Mt. Hikotake (355 meters) to partway up its slopes and near its summit. Most visitors stop at the Lower Shrine at the base, but those with the stamina and gear can follow the mountain trail up to the Middle and Upper Shrines. Near the summit, the view opens onto the farmland of Yamaga below — a landscape not unlike what ancient worshippers may have seen when they sensed divine power on this mountain.

A local legend still told today concerns the mountain's connection to the nearby rock formation Fudo-iwa. Mt. Hikotake and Fudo-iwa are said to have been half-brothers whose mother offered a sacred treasure to whichever of them won a tug-of-war. Mt. Hikotake (in his role as a mountain deity) won the contest, and the ground pushed up by their combined effort is said to have formed the small peak known as Yurugidake. Fudo-iwa is itself one of Yamaga's best-known scenic landmarks, and many visitors combine a trip there with a visit to Hikotake-gu.

## Visiting Tips

Hikotake-gu's shrine office, located at the Lower Shrine, is open 8:00–17:00. The grounds themselves can be visited at any hour, but goshuin and amulets are only available during office hours. Free parking is available near the Lower Shrine, but it holds only about 3–5 vehicles, and the approach road is narrow and lined with trees, so drivers of larger vehicles should take care, and visiting midday when the lot may already be full is best avoided. Since the shrine's limited-edition goshuin began attracting attention, weekends in particular tend to draw larger crowds, so an early-morning visit is recommended. Anyone planning to climb to the Middle or Upper Shrine should come prepared with proper hiking gear and allow enough time.

## Annual Festivals

- **Reisai (November 25, Lower Shrine)**: Hikotake-gu's most important annual festival.
- **Kenkokusai (February 11, Lower Shrine)**: A festival celebrating the founding of the nation, tied to Japan's founding mythology.
- **Tanemakisai (March 15, Upper Shrine)**: Held at planting time, praying for a bountiful harvest of the five grains.
- **Chugu-sai (April 8, Middle Shrine)**: A festival held at the Middle Shrine.
- **Niinamesai (November 23, Lower Shrine)**: A festival of thanksgiving for the year's harvest.

Beyond these, Hikotake-gu also observes Gantansai (New Year's Day), Genshisai (January 3), Kinensai (February 17), and Oharae purification rites (June 30 and December 29), with ceremonies carried out at the Upper, Middle, and Lower Shrines throughout the year.

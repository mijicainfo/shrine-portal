## History

The founding date of Ikune Jinja is unknown, but the shrine already appears in the Engishiki Jinmyocho of 927 as a kanpei-taisha, an official grand shrine receiving regular imperial offerings — clear evidence that it was an important sanctuary from very early times. According to shrine tradition, Ikune Jinja stood on this site even before neighboring Sumiyoshi Taisha was established, making it one of the oldest shrines in the old Sumiyoshi district. The enshrined deity, Sukunahikona no Mikoto, is revered as the ancestral god of sake brewing, and an old tradition holds that Empress Jingu brewed sake here to offer to the three Sumiyoshi deities. (A minority of historical sources, including the Shinmeishou Koushou, propose instead that the enshrined kami is Katsuhayahi Ame no Oshihomimi's brother deity Ikutsuhikone no Mikoto, though Sukunahikona no Mikoto is the deity recognized today.)

From the medieval period onward, Ikune Jinja came under the administration of Sumiyoshi Taisha together with its associated Buddhist temple, but it was restored to independent status as a village shrine (gosha) in 1872. Today it serves as the tutelary shrine (ubusuna-gami) for parishioners across parts of Sumiyoshi-ku and Suminoe-ku in Osaka. During the Toyotomi era in particular, the shrine was venerated by Yodo-dono, a consort of Toyotomi Hideyoshi, and the present main hall was built under her patronage around 1600–1601, with the retainer Katagiri Katsumoto serving as the construction magistrate. That main hall, preserving the Momoyama-period architectural style in complete form, is now designated an Important Cultural Property of Osaka Prefecture.

The shrine's popular nickname, Oku no Tenjin ("the inner Tenjin"), is said to derive either from its location further back from Sumiyoshi Taisha, or from "Oki no Amatsu-kami," an alternate name for Sukunahikona no Mikoto. Note that a separate, unrelated shrine also called Ikune Jinja (nicknamed Kami no Tenjin) exists in the Tamade area of Osaka's Nishinari Ward; the Sumiyoshi shrine's own official website explicitly advises visitors not to confuse the two, especially when arranging a prayer service.

## Highlights

The main hall (honden), rebuilt around 1600 through the patronage of Yodo-dono, is the shrine's foremost highlight. Built in the nagare-zukuri style with a gabled chidori-karahafu porch, a cypress-bark roof, and elaborate polychrome decoration, it preserves the Momoyama-period architectural style in complete form and is designated an Important Cultural Property of Osaka Prefecture. The worship hall (haiden), constructed of Kiso cypress and renovated in 1936, likewise carries strong Momoyama-era design influences.

Within the precincts stands the Tenmangu sub-shrine, home to a wooden seated statue of Sugawara no Michizane said to have been carved in 1479 and now designated a Tangible Cultural Property of Osaka City — a testament to the depth of devotion this shrine has drawn over the centuries. Several smaller shrines also stand on the grounds, including Ryuo-sha, Sae-no-kami-sha, Tanekashi Jinja, and an Inari shrine (Tenjo Inari Okami), and a building known as the Kobai-den is said to have been relocated from Shinra-ji, the Buddhist temple once attached to Sumiyoshi Taisha. Standing immediately north of Sumiyoshi Taisha's own grounds, Ikune Jinja offers a quieter, more contemplative sense of an old and dignified shrine.

## Visiting Tips

The grounds themselves can be visited freely, but the shrine office — for goshuin and amulets — is open 9:00 AM–5:00 PM. Goshuin are hand-written on request, and seasonal limited editions are sometimes offered, but there is no mail-order service, so a visit in person is required. On-site parking is available for worshippers, though anyone driving should be mindful that the surrounding roads can become congested during Sumiyoshi Taisha's own busy New Year and festival periods, given how close the two shrines sit to each other. A restroom is located on the west side of the grounds. Many visitors combine a stop here with a visit to Sumiyoshi Taisha just to the south.

## Annual Festivals

- **Saitansai (January 1)**: The first festival of the new year.
- **Setsubun Festival (February)**: A rite for warding off misfortune held around the traditional turn of the season.
- **Awashima Festival (April 1)**: A festival honoring the Awashima deity.
- **Tanabata Festival (July 7)**: The star festival, marked with its own rites.
- **Summer Festival Eve and procession (July 18–19)**: The summer festival's eve is followed the next day by a formal mikoshi procession (togyoshiki).
- **Reisai (October 9)**: The shrine's most important annual festival, held together with a mikoshi procession and a children's festival.
- **Shichi-Go-San visits (November)**: A season of visits to mark children's growth milestones.
- **Daikon-daki and New Year's Eve Festival (December)**: A rite of simmered daikon radish, followed by the joyasai on December 31 to close out the year.

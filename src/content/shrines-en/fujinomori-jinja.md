## History

Fujinomori Shrine stands in Fukakusa, Fushimi Ward, Kyoto. The official site bills it as a "shrine of victory luck and horses" and introduces it as a protector of the south of Heian-kyo and the local guardian shrine of Fukakusa, revered from the imperial court to samurai and ordinary people. According to the shrine's own account, Empress Jingu, after returning in triumph from Silla in the 3rd year of her regency (203), designated this place a sacred land of the kami, built a flag mound (hata-zuka) and enshrined the deities there (the top page says it was founded about 1,800 years ago). All of this is the shrine's own explanation, not something this page has confirmed as historical fact. The official site gives "Fujinomori Festival" the reading "Fujinomori Matsuri," so the shrine's name is also read "Fujinomori."

The official history also records that in 794 (Enryaku 13) Emperor Kanmu bestowed on it the title "Yuhei Mandokoro," and that an offering ceremony held in 863 (Jogan 5) at the time of Emperor Seiwa's accession is said to be the origin of the Fujinomori Festival.

According to the official site, the central seat of the main hall enshrines seven deities: Susanoo no Mikoto, Wakeikazuchi no Mikoto, Yamato Takeru no Mikoto, Emperor Ojin, Emperor Nintoku, Empress Jingu and Takeuchi no Sukune. The east seat holds Prince Toneri and Emperor Tenmu, and the west seat holds Prince Sawara, Prince Iyo and Princess Inoue. Prince Toneri is said to be the compiler of the Nihon Shoki and is also revered as a deity of learning and the arts, the official site explains.

## Highlights

According to the official grounds guide, the main hall is a building that was the Naishidokoro (Kashikodokoro) in the imperial palace and was granted by Emperor Nakamikado in 1712 (Shotoku 2); it is a Kyoto City designated cultural property. The worship hall was also granted from the palace and is a "wari-haiden" with a passage through the center. The current buildings of the auxiliary shrines Hachimangu-sha and Daishogun-sha date from 1438 (Eikyo 10) and are national Important Cultural Properties. Daishogun-sha enshrines Iwanagahime no Mikoto, a deity who protects against bad directions, and is described as the southern guardian of the shrines placed at the four sides of the capital at the time of the move to Heian-kyo.

The flag mound (hata-zuka) in the grounds is said to be where Empress Jingu raised her banner and enshrined the deities, and the adjacent Ichii-gashi oak ("Ichinoki-san") is venerated for relief from back pain; Kondo Isami is said to have visited in the late Edo period and been cured. Horses appear throughout the grounds: the spout of the hand-washing basin is a horse figure, and a sacred-horse statue symbolizes the Kakeuma ritual. The water called "Fuji no Mizu," said to rise from about 100 meters underground, is said to bring victory luck. The grounds also hold a Seven Lucky Gods statue group, a monument to Prince Toneri, a Kintaro statue (described as symbolizing the place where the Iris Festival, or "Shobu no Sekku," is said to have originated), and part of the stone walls of Fushimi Castle. The treasure hall is introduced as housing an Important Cultural Property, a suit of armor laced with purple silk (Murasaki-ito-odoshi o-yoroi). Viewing hours for the treasure hall are not stated on the official site.

## Visiting Tips

Visiting hours and shrine office hours are 9:00 AM–5:00 PM (official site). Prayer services need no booking, are accepted 9:00 AM–4:00 PM except during ceremonies, and start from ¥5,000. Prayer categories listed include victory, horse victory (kachiuma), exam success, traffic safety and protection from misfortune.

The official amulet list includes Kakeuma-mamori (¥1,000), Kachiuma-mamori (¥1,000), a victory-luck amulet (¥800), an exam-success amulet (¥800), Toneri academic amulet (¥1,000) and a back-pain amulet (¥800). For goshuin, the shrine's notice says that in 2026, a Year of the Horse, it has offered a "gold-leaf hidari-uma" since the start of the year, with a new seal added for a special November edition at ¥1,000. No hours are given for goshuin, so an earlier visit is safer.

It is a 5-minute walk from Fujinomori Station (JR Nara Line) and 7 minutes from Sumizome Station (Keihan Main Line). The official site gives no parking information for ordinary cars; tour buses must reserve by phone at least a week ahead. Public transport is the safer choice.

## Annual Festivals

Main events from the official annual-events list. Dates are as listed on the official site and may change from year to year.

- **Fujinomori Festival (May 1–5)**: The spring festival. The official site says it was once called the "Fukakusa Jogan Festival" and is "said to be the festival where the Iris Festival (Shobu no Sekku) originated" (the shrine's explanation, not presented as established history). On May 3 the divine spirits are transferred into the portable shrines, and on May 5 the Shinko-sai has portable shrines, a costumed procession and a fife-and-drum corps go around the parish.
- **Kakeuma Shinji (May 5)**: Held at the riding ground along the south approach at 1:00 PM and 3:00 PM. The Kakeuma Preservation Society performs feats such as "Fuji-sagari," "Tazuna-kuguri" and "Ichiji-gaki" on galloping horses.
- **Hydrangea Festival (June 15)**: Listed in the annual-events list. Opening dates and admission for the hydrangea garden were not stated on the pages checked, so check the official announcements.
- **Nagoshi-no-Oharae and Chinowa ritual (June 30, from 3:00 PM)**
- **Autumn Grand Festival and Hitaki-sai (November 5, from 10:00 AM)**
- **Shinshin-sai (second or third Monday of November)**: Described as a gathering of people who love horses and horse racing; in 2026 it is on Monday, November 9 at noon. After the ceremony there is a draw for shikishi boards signed by jockeys, according to the official notice.

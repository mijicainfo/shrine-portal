## History

Okama Jinja is a subsidiary shrine of Shiogama Jinja (Shiwahiko Jinja and Shiogama Jinja), known as the ichinomiya, or highest-ranked shrine, of the old province of Mutsu. Like the main shrine, it enshrines Shiotsuchi no Oji no Kami, a deity traditionally said to have taught the local people the art of making salt from seawater. The exact date of its founding is unknown, but the area was once known as Hode-no-Hama, a beach associated with ancient salt production, and tradition holds that Shiotsuchi no Oji no Kami taught the method of boiling seawater here. The place name "Shiogama" — literally "salt cauldron" — is itself said to derive from the sacred cauldrons enshrined at this site.

Inside the shrine hall stand four iron cauldrons known as shinkama, or "sacred cauldrons." Their water is said to never overflow or run dry, and legend holds that its color changes whenever something extraordinary is about to happen in the world; during the Edo period, the shrine is said to have been required to report any such color change to the rulers of the Sendai domain. Together with the "Ishi-no-Hoden" stone monument at Oushiko Jinja in Takasago, Hyogo Prefecture, and the "Amanosakahoko" spear at Kirishima Togu in Miyazaki Prefecture, these cauldrons are counted among the "Three Mysteries of Japan" (Nihon Sanki), a classification first proposed by the Edo-period physician and traveler Tachibana Nankei. The haiku poet Matsuo Basho is also recorded as having visited and paid his respects to the sacred cauldrons during his travels, an episode noted in his travelogue Oku no Hosomichi (The Narrow Road to the Deep North).

## Highlights

The main attraction is the shrine's four sacred cauldrons, housed inside the shrine hall. For a small viewing fee (¥100), visitors can view them up close and see the water itself. The legend that the water changes color before disaster strikes is a long-documented tradition, recorded as far back as the Edo period. In more recent years, it has often been said that the water appeared to change color on the day of the 2011 Great East Japan Earthquake and tsunami — but it's worth noting clearly that this is not a scientifically verified event, only an anecdote passed down and widely repeated rather than an established fact.

Though the grounds are modest in size, Okama Jinja sits just a short walk from Shiogama Jinja's main approach, carrying an air of quiet mystery befitting one of Japan's "Three Mysteries." Every July, the shrine's Moshioyaki salt-making ritual lets visitors witness an ancient method of salt production using seaweed, a tradition closely tied to the deity Shiotsuchi no Oji no Kami enshrined here.

## Visiting Tips

Because no calligrapher is stationed on-site, only pre-written goshuin are available at Okama Jinja itself; visitors wanting a goshuin written to order should ask at the main Shiogama Jinja's goshuin office instead. The sacred cauldrons can be viewed 6:00–18:00 from March to October and 6:00–17:00 from November to February, with no closed days — but it's worth allowing extra time, as viewing may not always be possible during certain hours. There is no parking for visitors on the grounds, so anyone arriving by car should use a nearby coin-operated lot. Since Okama Jinja is only a few minutes' walk from Shiogama Jinja's main approach, visiting both together is recommended.

## Annual Festivals

- **Moshioyaki Salt-Making Ritual (July 4–6)**: A ritual reenacting the ancient method of salt production, beginning with the harvest of hondawara seaweed, continuing with refilling the sacred cauldrons with seawater drawn at high tide, and concluding with boiling that seawater down with the seaweed to produce salt offered to the deity. Designated an Intangible Folk Cultural Property of Miyagi Prefecture.
- **Shiogama Jinja Annual Festival (July 10)**: The main annual festival of Shiogama Jinja, the shrine to which Okama Jinja belongs; Okama Jinja is considered closely connected to the rites of this festival as well.

## History

Gooh Jinja enshrines Wake no Kiyomaro, the courtier who advised Emperor Kanmu to relocate the capital to Heian-kyo (present-day Kyoto) and played a major role in its construction, along with his sister Wake no Hiromushi, who is remembered for taking in and raising orphaned children left parentless by war.

The exact founding date is unknown, but shrine tradition traces its origins to a shrine called Goo-zenjinja, once located within the grounds of Jingo-ji Temple on Mt. Takao in the western hills of Kyoto, an area closely associated with the Wake clan. As Kiyomaro's reputation as a key figure in the founding of the Heian capital grew, the imperial court bestowed the shrine's current name, Gooh Jinja ("Shrine of the Protector King"), in 1874. In 1886, by order of the Meiji Emperor, the shrine was relocated from Jingo-ji to its present site, directly across from the Hamaguri Gate on the west side of the Kyoto Imperial Palace, and was elevated to the prestigious rank of Bekkaku Kanpeisha, reflecting its role in guarding the imperial precincts.

Kiyomaro's connection to wild boars stems from a dramatic episode late in the Nara period. When the Buddhist priest Dokyo attempted to seize the imperial throne, Kiyomaro delivered an oracle contradicting Dokyo's ambitions, enraging him. As punishment, Kiyomaro was exiled to Osumi Province (in present-day Kagoshima) and his leg tendons were cut to prevent his return. As he was being transported into exile, badly wounded, some 300 wild boars are said to have suddenly appeared, surrounding his palanquin and safely guiding him along the road. Remarkably, Kiyomaro's severe leg injury is said to have begun healing in the aftermath of this event.

## Highlights

The single most distinctive feature of Gooh Jinja is its wild boar (inoshishi) imagery, found throughout the grounds. Rather than the pair of guardian lion-dogs (koma-inu) found at most Shinto shrines, Gooh Jinja has stationed a pair of guardian wild boar statues (koma-inoshishi) in front of its main hall since 1890, a rare and memorable sight that has earned it the popular nickname "Inoshishi Jinja," the Wild Boar Shrine.

At the purification fountain, known as the Reicho Chozusha, stands a bronze wild boar statue called the "Miraculous Boar of Good Fortune," from whose mouth clean water flows for ritual purification. Many visitors touch its snout in hopes of receiving blessings for leg and hip health. Elsewhere on the grounds, visitors can find the "Monument to Recovery from Leg Ailments," commemorating the healing of Kiyomaro's injury; a large collection of wild boar figurines and ornaments donated by worshippers from across the country; a sacred gingko tree honored as one of Kamigyo Ward's notable trees; and a Chinese quince tree associated with relief from asthma. A bronze statue of Kiyomaro himself stands on raised ground within the grounds, a dignified tribute to his role in founding the Heian capital.

## Visiting Tips

Gooh Jinja's location directly across from the Hamaguri Gate, on the western edge of Kyoto Gyoen (the Kyoto Imperial Palace park), makes it an easy and worthwhile stop when exploring the park. The shrine grounds are relatively compact, so visitors can comfortably take in the koma-inoshishi statues, the Miraculous Boar fountain, and the Monument to Recovery from Leg Ailments in about 30 minutes.

Because the shrine is known nationwide for blessings related to leg and hip health and recovery from injury or surgery, many visitors come to pray on behalf of athletes or elderly family members. The goshuin office is open from 9:30 to 16:30, and a wide variety of wild-boar-themed amulets and charms are available, so it's worth allowing extra time to browse. The shrine is especially busy during the Year of the Boar in the Chinese zodiac, so visiting earlier in the day is advisable if you'd prefer to avoid crowds.

## Annual Festivals

- **Inoko Festival (November 1)**: Reviving a Heian-era court custom, this festival involves preparing and offering rice cakes called inoko-mochi, followed by a procession in which participants carry a portable shrine chest to the Kyoto Imperial Palace.
- **Gooh Grand Festival (April 4)**: Held on the anniversary of Kiyomaro's death, this annual festival features a procession of parishioners and worshippers, including decorative umbrellas and wild-boar motifs, making its way to the Kyoto Imperial Palace.
- **Nagoshi Purification and Chinowa Ring-Passing (June)**: A purification ritual marking the halfway point of the year, in which visitors pass through a large ring made of woven reeds set up on the grounds to pray for continued good health through the second half of the year.

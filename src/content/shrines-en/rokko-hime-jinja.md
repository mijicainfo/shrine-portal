## History

Rokko Hime Jinja (formally Rokko Hime Daizen Jinja) sits quietly in a ravine deep in the mountains of Nada Ward, Kobe, Hyogo Prefecture. Its object of worship is not a conventional shrine building but the massive granite rock formation — an iwakura, or sacred rock — that rises behind it, making this one of the more distinctive surviving examples of old Japanese mountain-worship tradition.

According to shrine tradition, the monk Hodo Shonin, said to have traveled to Japan from India, was practicing austerities in the Mount Rokko mountains in the 7th century when he experienced a vision of the guardian deity Bishamonten appearing amid a purple cloud. This experience led him to found Kisshoin Tamonji temple in what is now Kita Ward, Kobe, and the rock formation here was established as the temple's inner sanctuary (oku-no-in), remaining an object of devotion ever since.

The shrine's deity, Rokko Hime Daizen, is identified in Buddhist tradition with Benzaiten and Kisshoten. In 2012, Rokko Hime Ko — the lay association that helps maintain the shrine — publicly taught, based on its own study of the Hotsuma Tsutae (a text written in a purported ancient script), that this deity is one and the same as Seoritsuhime, a Shinto deity associated with water and purification. It's worth noting that this identification does not come from the classical Kojiki or Nihon Shoki, but is a comparatively recent teaching. In the same spirit, some tellings hold that the rock formation itself was artificially stacked by human hands during the Jomon period (around 5000 BCE) — an intriguing idea, but not one confirmed by archaeological evidence, and presented here as a belief that circulates among visitors rather than settled fact.

Documented physical rebuildings of the shrine include new shrine buildings and stone steps in 1968, a completed worship hall and approach stairway on November 20, 1996, and a rebuilt shrine hall on September 29, 2019. Today the site is jointly maintained by Tamonji temple and Rokko Hime Ko, with joint rites held every autumn.

## Highlights

The individual boulders that make up the iwakura each carry their own name. The three most notable are Kumo-ga-iwa ("Cloud Rock"), where Hodo Shonin is said to have experienced his vision of Bishamonten; Shingyo-iwa ("Heart Sutra Rock"), inscribed with the Buddhist Heart Sutra; and Gyoga-iwa ("Reclining Rock"), which sits at the top of the formation. All are enormous blocks of Rokko granite, marked by the columnar jointing typical of the stone. The small shrine hall is tucked into the narrow ravine as if cradled by these boulders, giving visitors a distinct sense of quiet tension the moment they arrive.

In recent years the shrine has increasingly been featured as a "power spot," drawing visitors from well beyond the immediate area on weekends. Even so, reaching the shrine hall requires descending a steep stairway and mountain trail, and signage along the way is minimal — so the atmosphere remains that of a quiet mountain-worship site rather than a developed tourist destination.

## Visiting Tips

Rokko Hime Jinja has no resident priest or shrine office — it is entirely unstaffed. Ema (about ¥300), amulets (about ¥700), and pre-printed goshuin are left near the worship hall on a self-service, leave-a-donation basis, technically available around the clock. That said, the approach involves a steep mountain trail and stairway, so visiting after dark or in bad weather is strongly discouraged — plan to arrive in daylight, wearing shoes suited to a hike. If you're relying on public transit, build in enough time to make the round trip within the operating hours of the Rokko Cable and mountain bus. In winter, check trail conditions in advance, as snow and ice are possible.

## Annual Festivals

- **Autumn Grand Festival**: Every autumn, Tamonji temple (in Kita Ward, Kobe) and its inner sanctuary, Rokko Hime Jinja, hold a joint rite. The exact date varies from year to year, so those planning around it should confirm details with Tamonji or Rokko Hime Ko directly.

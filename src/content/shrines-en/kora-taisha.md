## History

Kora Taisha stands on Mount Kora (elevation 312 m) in Kurume, Fukuoka Prefecture, and has long been revered as the ichinomiya, or chief shrine, of former Chikugo Province. Shrine tradition dates its founding to the first year of Emperor Richu's reign, traditionally placed at 400 CE, though other traditions instead place it in the 55th or 78th year of Emperor Nintoku's reign. One founding legend holds that the deity Takagi no Kami originally resided on Mount Kora, and that Kora Tamatare no Mikoto later marked out a sacred boundary and settled there in the deity's place.

The shrine was ranked among the Myojin shrines in 818 and granted the senior first court rank in 897. It appears in the 10th-century Engishiki shrine register as "Kora Tamatare no Mikoto Jinja" of Mii District, Chikugo Province, ranked as a Myojin Taisha — a designation that shows how highly the Imperial court regarded it as the preeminent shrine of Chikugo Province from the Heian period onward. In 1871 it was ranked a Kokuhei Chusha, and in 1915 elevated to Kokuhei Taisha. Since the postwar reorganization of Shinto administration, it has held the status of a beppyo shrine under the Association of Shinto Shrines, maintaining its standing as one of Kyushu's foremost ancient shrines to this day.

The main sanctuary enshrines Kora Tamatare no Mikoto, with Hachiman Okami in the left sanctuary and Sumiyoshi Okami in the right; Toyohime Okami is also enshrined within the honden. Kora Tamatare no Mikoto is a deity absent from both the Kojiki and Nihon Shoki, and the identity behind the name has been debated since ancient times — theories include Takeuchi no Sukune, the close retainer of Empress Jingu; an ancestral deity of the Mononobe clan; and Azumi no Isora, a deity associated with seafaring clans. This unresolved, enigmatic origin adds a distinctive depth to the shrine's tradition of worship.

The slopes of Mount Kora also hold ruins far older than the shrine itself: the Kora-san Kogoishi, an ancient mountain fortification. Believed to date from roughly the 6th to 7th century, it consists of massive cut stones laid end to end across two valleys, running about 1.5 km and comprising some 1,300 stones. It is generally thought to have been built by the Yamato court to defend northern Kyushu amid the tense diplomatic climate before and after the Battle of Hakusukinoe in 663, when Yamato forces were defeated by the combined armies of Tang China and Silla, and it is now a National Historic Site. Notably, the very term "kogoishi" originated from this site on Mount Kora, after scholars first reported its discovery here; similar ruins later found elsewhere in Japan came to be called by the same name.

## Highlights

The current honden, heiden, and haiden were built between 1660 and 1661, funded by Arima Yoritoshi, the third lord of the Kurume domain. Built in an irimoya style with cypress-bark shingled roofing and an elaborate three-tiered bracket system supporting a railed veranda, the complex is one of the largest shrine building groups in all of Kyushu. The stone grand torii at the entrance to the approach was donated in 1655 by Arima Tadayori, the domain's second lord, and together with the honden, heiden, and haiden was designated an Important Cultural Property of Japan in 1972.

The main approach features a 131-step stone staircase known as "Honzaka," rising at roughly a 30-degree grade with a 20-meter change in elevation, with views over the city of Kurume from partway up. For visitors who find the stairs difficult, a paid, shared slope car runs alongside the staircase, carrying passengers up to the shrine grounds in about ninety seconds. Further beyond the main precinct, a path through the forest behind the honden leads to the Okumiya (inner shrine), where a Bishamon hall said to have been founded in 678 and the Mitarai Pond — where, tradition holds, the deities once washed their hands — offer a quieter setting for worship. The grounds are also known for a grove of moso kinmei bamboo, a species designated a National Natural Monument and found in only a handful of locations nationwide, as well as a pair of sacred sakaki trees associated with matchmaking blessings.

## Visiting Tips

The grounds can be visited at any hour, but the shrine office handling prayer requests and goshuin is open 9:00–16:00. Six free parking lots (Lots 1–6) are available on the grounds, with Lots 5 and 6, near the moso kinmei bamboo grove, offering especially generous space. The New Year period and the October Kora-san Kunchi festival draw large crowds, so visitors driving up are advised to allow extra time. The shrine's observation deck offers sweeping views over the city of Kurume and the Chikugo Plain, and is also a popular spot for night views.

## Annual Festivals

- **Kawatari Festival (also known as "Hekokaki Matsuri," June 1–2)**: A midsummer purification rite in which participants pass through a ring of woven reeds to cleanse half a year's accumulated impurities; at Kora Taisha it is especially known by its local nickname, the Hekokaki Matsuri.
- **Reitaisai Grand Festival / Kora-san Kunchi (October 9–11)**: Kora Taisha's most important festival, held over three days with a divine procession and a variety of ceremonies and dedicatory events.
- **Goshinki Festival (once every 50 years)**: A special grand festival held once every half-century; in 1992 it was celebrated as the 1,600th-anniversary Goshinki Grand Festival.

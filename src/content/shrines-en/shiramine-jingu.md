## History

Shiramine Jingu was founded on September 6, 1868, the first year of the Meiji era, to enshrine the spirit of Emperor Sutoku, who died in despair after being exiled to Sanuki Province (present-day Sakaide, Kagawa Prefecture) following his defeat in the Hogen Rebellion. After his death, a series of great fires and conflicts struck the capital, and Emperor Sutoku's spirit came to be feared as the source of this misfortune. Carrying out the wishes of his father, Emperor Komei, Emperor Meiji ordered that Sutoku's spirit be brought back from his distant mausoleum at Shiramine in Sanuki and pacified in Kyoto as a new era began, and a shrine was built for this purpose on the former residence of the Asukai family, court nobles renowned as hereditary masters of kemari and waka poetry. The shrine's name, "Shiramine," comes directly from the name of Emperor Sutoku's mausoleum, Shiramine no Misasagi.

In 1873, the spirit of Emperor Junnin was also brought from his mausoleum in Awaji and enshrined alongside Sutoku. Junnin, too, had been exiled — to Awaji Province, after being implicated in a political conspiracy — and died there without ever returning to the capital. Because both emperors were driven from Kyoto by political defeat and met tragic ends, reverently enshrining and pacifying their spirits stands as the central purpose behind the shrine's founding.

## Highlights

The on-site Jishu-sha sub-shrine enshrines Seidaimyojin, the guardian deity of kemari that the Asukai family had worshipped within their own residence for generations. Kemari, a Heian-period pastime in which players keep a deerskin ball aloft using only their feet, is often described as an ancestor of modern ball sports played on the ground, and Seidaimyojin has accordingly come to be revered not only as the guardian of kemari but of soccer, ball games in general, and sports as a whole.

Beside the shrine office, visitors will find numerous soccer balls and autographed shikishi boards donated by prominent athletes, including members of the Japan national team and players from the J.League and V.League, alongside an official match ball used at the FIFA World Cup. The worship hall displays autographs from famous athletes and banners cheering on Japan's national team, and the shrine has even received visits from professional soccer club executives attending its festivals — a testament to its deep ties with the world of sports.

## Visiting Tips

On the shrine grounds stands a stone ball known as "nade-mari" (stroking ball); tradition holds that spinning the ball on top with your hand while wishing for good fortune in ball games brings improvement in sports and victory in competition. Beyond athletes praying for success, the shrine is well known as a pilgrimage site for soccer fans, and it draws large crowds before and after Japan national team matches.

## Annual Festivals

- **Spring Grand Festival / Emperor Junnin Festival (April 14)**: A festival honoring Emperor Junnin. Following the main ritual, the Kemari Preservation Society offers an elegant kemari performance starting around 10:30 AM.
- **Seidaimyojin Festival / Tanabata Festival (July 7)**: A festival honoring Seidaimyojin, featuring a kemari offering around 1:30 PM and a "Komachi Odori" dance performance around 4:00 PM.

## Nearby Food & Sightseeing

About a 5-minute walk from the shrine, near the Ichijo Modoribashi bridge, stands Seimei Shrine, dedicated to the legendary onmyoji Abe no Seimei — a popular spot many visitors combine with their visit here. Near Karasuma-Ichijo, the historic wagashi maker Toraya, founded in the late Muromachi period, runs a tea house well suited to a rest after your visit.

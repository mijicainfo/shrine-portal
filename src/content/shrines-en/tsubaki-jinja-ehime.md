## History

Tsubaki Jinja — officially Iyozuhiko-no-Mikoto Jinja — enshrines four kami: Iyozuhiko-no-Mikoto, Iyozuhime-no-Mikoto, Iyonushi-no-Mikoto, and Ehime-no-Mikoto. Known locally since ancient times as "Tsubaki Jinja" or "O-Tsubaki-san," shrine tradition holds that it was founded during the reign of the 7th Emperor Korei, roughly 2,300 years ago, making it one of the oldest shrines anywhere in Shikoku.

According to the shrine's founding legend, the two kami Iyozuhiko-no-Mikoto and Iyozuhime-no-Mikoto arrived by boat at a hill on what was then a stretch of coastline reaching inland to this very spot. There, a local deity, Shionari-Takutsuna-no-Okina-no-Kami, tied their boat's mooring rope to welcome them ashore. The name "Tsubaki" (camellia), used for both the shrine and the surrounding area, is explained two ways in local tradition: as a corruption of "tsu-no-waki" ("beside the harbor," reflecting the site's coastal origins), or as a reference to the camellia trees that grew wild here.

Of the four enshrined kami, Ehime-no-Mikoto is the deity for whom Ehime Prefecture itself was named when the modern prefecture was established in the Meiji era — reportedly the only case in Japan of a prefecture taking its name directly from an enshrined deity.

## Highlights

The subsidiary Komori Jinja, on the shrine grounds, is dedicated to watching over children's healthy growth, and is known for the custom of "toshi-daiko," in which a child beats a drum once for each year of their age when visiting. Standing nearby is a pair of wooden komainu (guardian lion-dogs) believed to date from the late Muromachi period, designated a Matsuyama City Tangible Cultural Property (sculpture) on March 25, 1977. Having watched over worshippers for centuries, they are among the grounds' most notable cultural treasures.

The other subsidiary shrine, Shogun Hachiman Jinja, holds its annual festival every January 15 and is known as a place to pray for victory and success. A distinctive feature of Tsubaki Jinja is that all three shrines — the main shrine plus Shogun Hachiman Jinja and Komori Jinja — each offer their own dedicated goshuin.

Camellia trees, the flowering namesake of the shrine, are planted throughout the grounds and its surroundings, offering visitors a seasonal splash of color tied directly to the shrine's own name.

## Visiting Tips

Visiting hours shift with the seasons — 7:00–17:00 in the winter months of January and February, stretching to around 6:00–18:00 from April through September as sunrise and sunset times change. The shrine office where goshuin and amulets are available generally opens around 7:30 and closes with the grounds, so it's worth allowing enough time.

After paying respects at the main hall, it's worth visiting the subsidiary Komori Jinja and Shogun Hachiman Jinja as well, and picking up their individual goshuin. The pair of wooden komainu near Komori Jinja, a designated Matsuyama City cultural property, is also worth a close look.

Most visitors arrive by Iyotetsu bus, though the shrine is a convenient 10–15 minute drive from either JR Matsuyama Station or Matsuyama Airport. During the Tsubaki Matsuri, held over the 7th–9th days of the lunar New Year, the prefectural road around the shrine is closed entirely to traffic and the parking lots close as well, so public transport is strongly recommended during that period.

## Annual Festivals

- **Tsubaki Matsuri (lunar New Year, days 7–9)**: Tsubaki Jinja's signature grand festival, held continuously for 72 hours from midnight. Known as "the festival that calls spring to Iyo," it draws roughly 500,000 visitors from across Shikoku and beyond, with about 800 stalls lining the road to the shrine. On the evening of the second day, a solemn, silent mikoshi procession called "Oshinobi no Togyo" takes place. In 2026 the festival runs February 23 (Mon, national holiday) through 25 (Wed).
- **Setsubun Festival (February 3)**: A ritual of bean-throwing and archery to drive away misfortune and pray for good luck.
- **Autumn Grand Festival (October 6–7)**: The main festival is held on October 6, followed on the morning of the 7th by the "Shinkosai" mikoshi procession and, in the evening, the "Kangosai" procession returning the mikoshi to the shrine.
- **Summer Purification (July 31)**: A ritual to purify half a year's accumulated impurities, held alongside a ceremony to lay old amulets and dolls to rest.
- **Shichigosan Festival (November 15)**: A celebration of children's growth and prayers for their healthy future.

## History

Namiyoke Inari Jinja was, according to tradition, founded in 1659, early in the Edo period. At the time, the city of Edo was expanding rapidly, and land reclamation work was underway throughout the Tsukiji area — but the project was repeatedly hampered by fierce winds and rough seas. One night, a mysterious object was seen drifting on the water, glowing softly; when the workers retrieved it and enshrined it as the manifestation of an Inari deity, holding a grand festival in its honor, the rough waves are said to have calmed at once, and the reclamation work proceeded smoothly from then on. In recognition of this miraculous protection, the shrine took its name, Namiyoke — "wave protection" or "warding off waves."

After Tokyo's central wholesale market relocated to Tsukiji following the 1923 Great Kanto Earthquake, Namiyoke Inari Jinja came to be deeply revered as the guardian shrine of the market and everyone connected to it. Its divine reputation for "warding off disaster and riding out the waves" resonated naturally with the market workers' own hopes for business prosperity and safety on the water, and the shrine remains today a spiritual home for people whose livelihoods are tied to food.

## Highlights

The single greatest highlight of Namiyoke Inari Jinja is the pair of enormous lion heads enshrined in the Shishiden hall on the grounds. The protective "Tenjo Odaijishi" and the black-toothed "Ohaguro Jishi" both stand over 2 meters tall, and every year around June the shrine's main festival, the Tsukiji Lion Festival, features a ceremonial procession carrying the giant lion heads out of the shrine — drawing large crowds of spectators. The main hall also preserves a smaller wooden lion head carved in 1848 by Shimaya Tojiro III, which miraculously survived the 1923 earthquake and is still enshrined with great care today.

Equally distinctive are the many stone monuments dedicated to food scattered throughout the grounds. Sushi-zuka, ebi-zuka (shrimp), anko-zuka (anglerfish), katsugyo-zuka (live fish), tamago-zuka (eggs), and konbu-zuka (kelp) — each was donated by a trade association connected to the Tsukiji market, offering thanks for a particular ingredient central to its members' livelihoods. Together they form a uniquely food-centered style of devotion found nowhere else quite like it, fitting for a shrine so closely tied to Tokyo's most famous food market.

## Visiting Tips

The grounds can be visited at any time, but the shrine office — for amulets and goshuin — is open 9:00–17:00. Because of its proximity to the Tsukiji Outer Market, many visitors combine a morning stroll through the market's food stalls with a stop at the shrine. There is no parking for visitors on the grounds, so anyone arriving by car should use the nearby Tsukijigawa No.1 parking lot or a coin-operated lot instead. The shrine is especially busy during the June Lion Festival, so visiting at a different time of year is recommended for a quieter experience.

## Annual Festivals

- **Tsukiji Lion Festival / main annual festival (around June)**: The shrine's biggest event of the year, featuring a ceremonial procession of the giant lion heads out of the shrine and drawing large crowds of spectators.
- **Memorial rites for sushi-zuka, ebi-zuka, and other food monuments (held by individual trade associations)**: Ceremonies of thanks and remembrance held before each stone monument by the trade association that dedicated it.
- **New Year visits (from January 1)**: Draws worshippers from across the Tsukiji and Nihonbashi areas praying for business prosperity in the year ahead.
